Brake, Throttle and Speed Readings From an Event Data Recorder

Many cars carry an event data recorder, a module that saves vehicle data when a crash triggers it. Federal rules in 49 CFR Part 563 do not require a carmaker to install one. They set what the recorder must capture when a car built on or after September 1, 2012, has one. Part 563's required owner's manual statement says the recorder stores no names and no crash location. Phone activity is not among its data elements either. The other car's recorder is worth asking about before a body shop or salvage buyer reaches it.

Five Seconds of Pre-Crash Data Under 49 CFR Part 563

Section 563.7 lists what a covered recorder must keep. The list includes the car's speed, how far the accelerator was pressed and whether the brake pedal was down. For cars on the road today, the rule calls for those readings twice a second across the last five seconds. A 2024 federal amendment stretches the window to 20 seconds, phased in for cars built starting September 1, 2028. Suppose a car rear-ends you near the I-26 exit for Ashley Phosphate Road. If every brake sample in those five seconds reads off, the recorder logged no braking before impact, though it cannot say why.

Who May Download the Recorder Under the Driver Privacy Act

The Driver Privacy Act of 2015, a federal law, makes recorder data the property of the vehicle's owner. For a leased car, the lessee owns it. Anyone else may retrieve the data only on a short list of grounds. Two of them matter in a crash claim.

Written or Recorded Consent From the Owner

An owner or lessee can agree to a download in writing, electronically or in a recorded statement. The owner of your own car can consent to reading your recorder. It may show you slowing and braking before the hit. The at-fault car's owner has little reason to agree, and nothing in the Act forces consent.

A Court Order Authorizing Retrieval

A court or other judicial or administrative body with jurisdiction can authorize the download. The Act adds that retrieved data must still meet that tribunal's standards for admission into evidence. That route usually opens after a lawsuit is filed. In the meantime, a lawyer can send the owner a letter asking that the car and its recorder stay unaltered.

Phone Company Logs and the Limits of 18 U.S.C. 2702

Carrier records can place a call or text at a minute that matches the crash time on the report. A federal statute, 18 U.S.C. 2702, bars a public messaging or phone provider from knowingly revealing the stored contents of a message. Its exceptions are narrow, such as consent from a sender or recipient. The same section lets a provider release customer records without content to anyone other than a government entity. So from the carrier, a crash claim usually gets time stamps, not words. Frost Law Group can line those times up with the report and any recorder data.

Does a Hands-Free Ticket Prove the Other Driver Was at Fault?

Not by itself. Section 56-5-3890 has barred holding a phone while driving since September 1, 2025. It labels a driver found in violation guilty of distracted driving. Compare the seat belt law. Section 56-5-6540(C) says a belt violation is not negligence per se, meaning fault established by the violation itself. It also bars using the violation as evidence in a civil case. The hands-free statute has no clause like that. How a court treats a hands-free citation turns on the evidence rules and the case facts, which a lawyer researches first.

Can the Recorder in Your Own Car Work Against You?

It can. Your car's recorder keeps the same speed, throttle and brake readings as the recorder in the other car. If it shows you over the posted limit or never braking, an insurer may use it to argue that you share the fault. The data belongs to your car's owner, so a lawyer talks the decision through with the owner before anyone downloads it.

What if Both Drivers Were Looking Away When They Collided?

Possible Injury and Suspected Minor Injury Codes

The Fact Book defines a possible injury as one a person reports or shows through behavior. No wound is readily evident. A suspected minor injury is one evident at the scene, such as a lump on the head, abrasions, bruises or small cuts. Neck or back pain after a rear-end crash would usually fall under the first code, since nothing shows.

Who Pays in a Three-Car Chain Collision on I-26?

Splitting a 25/50/25 Policy Among Several Injured People

South Carolina's floor for bodily injury liability, in Section 38-77-140, is $25,000 for any one person. The same section sets $50,000 for all people hurt in the same crash, plus $25,000 for property. The South Carolina Department of Insurance auto insurance page lists the same 25/50/25 floor. Suppose a minimum-limits driver on Ashley River Road looks down and hits a car carrying three people. All three draw on a single $50,000 pool, and none can take more than $25,000 of it. Passengers Hurt by the Driver They Rode With

Distraction is not always in the other car. A passenger hurt because the driver of that same car was texting may be able to claim against the liability policy covering that driver. Company Cars and Dispatch Screens

Section 56-5-3890 exempts a driver sending or receiving digital dispatch data while performing job duties. The exemption answers the hands-free question only, and it says nothing about the general duty to keep a lookout. A delivery driver who reads a dispatch screen and drifts into your car can still be careless under ordinary negligence rules. If the vehicle belonged to a business, that business's auto policy may also cover the crash, so the owner of the car matters to the claim.

Your Own UIM When the At-Fault Policy Runs Out

Underinsured motorist coverage, known as UIM, reaches damages past the at-fault driver's limits. Section 38-77-160 makes every insurer put it on offer, and the policyholder may accept or decline. Uninsured motorist coverage, or UM, is not optional. Section 38-77-150 writes it into every South Carolina auto policy. Your declarations page shows whether you bought UIM and the limits of both coverages.

Crash Force Data for Injuries an Insurer Disputes

The recorder that logs braking also measures the crash pulse. Part 563 calls it delta-V. It is the change in a car's velocity along its length over the first quarter second, sampled every hundredth of a second. An insurer may argue that a crash with modest damage could not have caused a serious injury. Recorder data gives an accident reconstructionist a measured figure to work from, while treating physicians address the medical side. The value of that data in a given claim turns on the car, the download and the injury.
