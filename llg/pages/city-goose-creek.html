What if the Other Driver Was a Federal Employee on Duty?

Then the Federal Tort Claims Act, the popular name for a chapter in Title 28 of the United States Code, may control the claim. Section 2679 makes a claim against the United States the only remedy when a federal employee acting within the scope of employment causes the injury. That generally rules out suing the driver personally. For a service member, Section 2671 defines acting within the scope of employment as acting in line of duty. Whether a driver met that test is a factual question, and the answer can reshape the claim.

The Written Claim and the Two-Year Federal Deadline

Section 2401 ties the claim to two deadlines. The written claim goes to the right federal agency within two years after it accrues. After the agency mails a final denial, a six-month window for filing suit begins. Under Section 2675, no lawsuit may start before that denial, though six months of agency silence may be treated as one. A later suit generally cannot ask for more than the amount claimed, barring newly discovered evidence or intervening facts.

Contractors, Service Members and the Employee Definition

Section 2671 counts members of the military forces as employees of the government. It also lists the military departments among federal agencies. Its definition of a federal agency leaves out any contractor with the United States. So a crash with a contractor's truck on Red Bank Road may proceed as an ordinary claim against the company and its insurer, depending on the contract and the driver's employer.

Military Medical Care and the Government's Recovery Right

Some crash victims receive care that the United States must or may provide or pay for by law. The Federal Medical Care Recovery Act, Section 2651 of Title 42, then gives the government its own right to recover the care's reasonable value. It may collect from the at-fault driver or that driver's insurer. If a hurt service member cannot perform regular military duties, the section also reaches the pay that accrued during that period. Proof That the Unknown Car Caused the Harm

Act No. 180 of 2024 rewrote Section 38-77-170, effective May 20, 2024. A claim against an unknown driver now needs one of three kinds of proof, and the recording route is new. Which route fits depends on the scene, and sometimes two apply at once.

Contact Damage or a Witness Who Signs

Paint transfer or a broken mirror housing from the fleeing car can show physical contact. Without contact, a person who neither owned nor drove your car can sign an affidavit, a sworn written account. If that person refuses, the statute lets you ask a court for a Rule 27 deposition before any suit is filed.

A Recording From a Dashcam or Storefront Camera

Since the rewrite, a dashcam clip or store video can qualify when it shows the unknown vehicle causing the harm. The statute accepts a recording made electronically or by other means. Some systems overwrite older clips, and a letter to the camera's owner can ask that the file be preserved.
