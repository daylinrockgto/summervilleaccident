Asking the Police Department for Its Copy

Moving the Cars After a Wreck on a Crowded Lane

Blocked lanes on a busy corridor create pressure to clear the road. South Carolina law answers that pressure differently depending on how badly someone got hurt. A lawyer looks at when and why each car moved, since an insurer may raise it later. Photos taken before the move often show where each car sat at impact, so take them if you safely can.

Damage-Only Wrecks Under Section 56-5-1220

When a crash damages only vehicles and one of them blocks traffic, Section 56-5-1220(B) requires each driver to make every reasonable effort to move a drivable car off the roadway. The statute also protects that choice. A driver who moves a car before an officer arrives is not liable or at fault for the crash just because of the move.

Serious Injury Wrecks Under Section 56-5-1210

A wreck that kills someone or leaves great bodily injury follows a stricter rule. Section 56-5-1210(B) bars moving the vehicle until the investigating officer authorizes it. The statute defines great bodily injury as harm carrying a substantial risk of death, serious permanent disfigurement, or a long-lasting loss of how a limb or organ works. The vehicles then stay where they came to rest until the officer says otherwise.

Following Distance on the Coleman to I-526 Stretch

Picture a driver on US 17 between Coleman Boulevard and I-526 who closes on stopped traffic and strikes the car ahead. Under Section 56-5-1930, the question is whether that driver's gap was reasonable and prudent. It weighs the speed of the cars, the density of traffic and the condition of the pavement. The Safety Check Section 56-5-1900 Requires

Signaling for the Final 100 Feet Under Section 56-5-2150

Section 56-5-2150 bars moving right or left unless the move is reasonably safe and properly signaled. The blinker has to stay on without a break across the final 100 feet before the turn. Breaking the section brings a $25 fine and adds no points to the driver's record.

Can a Driver Who Stopped Short Share the Blame?

Does a Missing Seat Belt Reduce a Mount Pleasant Claim?

Not under the belt law itself. Under Section 56-5-6540(C), a belt violation cannot be treated as negligence per se, meaning automatic proof of carelessness. An insurer may still ask about the belt, and the facts of each crash shape how that conversation goes. Child restraint rules carry a similar bar in Section 56-5-6460.
