What Lyft Accident Lawyers in Summerville Check Under the TNC Act

Section 58-23-1620 sits at the top of the list. No rideshare company may advertise or operate in South Carolina until the Office of Regulatory Staff grants it a permit. It must also meet the rest of Article 16. At Frost Law Group, a Lyft claim begins with a narrower question, though. Does this particular trip fall inside the Act at all?

The answer comes from the Act's definitions of a passenger, a personal vehicle and a prearranged ride. A crash outside those definitions follows ordinary auto insurance rules. The Permit and the Regulators Who Police It

The Office of Regulatory Staff issues the permit and keeps watch afterward. Section 58-23-1620(C) tells that office to balance innovative, safe and cost-effective transportation against safety protections for passengers and the public. Its staff may inspect driver qualification files and rideshare vehicles.

Under Section 58-23-1670(D), the company must give the office factual information about drivers, riders and trips on request, so it can investigate complaints. South Carolina law enforcement officers may enforce the Act's requirements, too. A regulatory finding does not decide an injury claim, but it can point to records worth requesting.

Cars and Trips the Act Leaves Out

The Act covers a personal vehicle that the driver owns, leases or has permission to use. A leased sedan or a spouse's SUV can qualify. Taxis, charter buses, limousines and other for-hire vehicles fall outside it. A rideshare vehicle may not have a rated seating capacity of more than eight, counting the driver.

The definition of a prearranged ride also excludes shared-expense carpools. It leaves out non-emergency medical rides arranged for Medicaid or Medicare members under a contract with the State or a managed care organization. Someone hurt on one of those trips may face a different set of insurance rules.

Does the TNC Act Call Lyft a Common Carrier?

The Act never uses the term, and Article 16 does not say whether rideshare companies are common carriers. It does set them apart from the taxi and motor carrier rules in Articles 1 through 15 of Chapter 23.

Under Section 58-23-1710(A), Article 16 and the regulator's rules govern rideshare companies and drivers exclusively, unless the chapter says otherwise. Local traffic and parking ordinances still apply. None of that regulatory language settles whether a rideshare company is a common carrier. The same article governs Uber, and Frost Law Group applies the same list of duties to Uber crash claims too.

The figures in the statute are floors, and a company's actual policy may carry more. The Logged-On Tier and the Accepted-Ride Tier

The lower tier begins the moment a driver goes online and waits for a match. Subsection (B) then calls for at least $50,000 for each person injured or killed and $100,000 for each incident. The property damage minimum doubles the personal-policy figure, to $50,000 instead of $25,000.

Accepting a request lifts the floor to $1 million under subsection (C). That amount covers death, injury and property damage together, as one limit shared by everyone the crash harms. It starts at acceptance, not at login or pickup, and lasts until the last requesting rider steps out.

Who Carries the Coverage and Which Insurer Pays First

The driver, the company or both may supply each tier. Section 58-23-1625(A) lets a driver's personal insurer exclude every coverage in its policy during app time. Subsection (D) of Section 58-23-1630 answers that exclusion.

A lapsed personal policy, or one without the required coverage, shifts the claim to the company's insurance. That insurance must respond starting with the first dollar and must defend the claim. Subsection (E) bars that insurer from making you wait for a personal insurer's denial. The Act also lets an eligible surplus lines insurer write the policy, so the carrier on a letter may be one you have never heard of.

How a Summerville Lyft Passenger Injury Lawyer Handles Fault and Seat Belt Questions

Can a rider in the back seat of a Lyft share the blame for a crash? Usually the fault dispute runs between the drivers, not the rider. South Carolina follows modified comparative negligence, so jurors assign each party a share of fault by percentage. If the injured person's share tops 50%, the claim fails. At 50% or less, the award drops in proportion to that person's own share.

Does the 2025 Tort Reform Act Reach Your Lyft Claim?

That turns on when the claim arose. Act No. 42 of 2025 applies only when the claim arose or accrued at some point after January 1, 2026. A crash from 2025 keeps the older rules, even when suit comes later.

One rule stays the same either way. Under Section 15-38-15, a defendant the jury finds under 50% at fault pays only its own portion of the damages. That has been true since 2005. What Act No. 42 changed is the list of people whose fault the jury weighs, which now includes wrongdoers nobody sued. It also ended full liability based only on alcohol or gross negligence. Seat Belts in the Back Seat of a Lyft

Section 56-5-6520 requires the driver and every occupant to wear a fastened safety belt on South Carolina's public roads. That includes the rear seat of a Lyft. The driver must also see that each occupant 17 or younger buckles up or rides in a child restraint, unless that teen holds a license or permit. A violation brings a fine of no more than $25. Section 56-5-6540(A) says it is not a criminal offense, and it stays off the driving record and out of reports to the rider's insurer.

What Section 56-5-6540(C) Keeps Out of an Injury Case

Subsection (C) settles the question in one sentence. Negligence per se means treating the breach of a safety law as carelessness in itself. Contributory negligence refers to blame that falls on the injured person.

Under the statute, failing to buckle is neither one, and the violation "is not admissible as evidence in a civil action." So the defense may not put your unbuckled belt in front of a jury as evidence in your injury case. Frost Law Group cites the statute whenever an adjuster brings up the belt.
