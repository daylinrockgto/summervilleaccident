Rider Injuries That Each Need Their Own Records

One motorcycle crash can damage skin, bone and the nervous system at the same moment. Different records prove each kind of injury, and they come from different points in the rider's care. Frost Law Group can sort the chart by injury, giving each diagnosis its own line of proof. That sorting also answers an insurer who blames one injury on something other than the crash.

Road Rash and Photos From Each Dressing Change

Road rash is skin torn away by pavement. It ranges from a surface scrape to a deep wound that needs repeated cleaning, and a deep wound may need a skin graft. Photos from each dressing change show how far the wound went and how it healed. A scar photographed long after the crash can look minor to an adjuster who never saw the open wound.

Broken Bones, Surgical Hardware and Operative Reports

A rider thrown from a bike may break a leg, an arm, the pelvis or a collarbone. The operative report lists each plate, rod or screw a surgeon placed, and imaging shows the fracture before and after repair. Hardware sometimes has to come out in a later operation. That future surgery counts in the claim after a treating physician documents the need for it.

Head and Spine Injuries With Symptoms That Surface Later

Some brain and spinal injuries do not show clearly in the emergency department. A rider may first notice dizziness, confusion or tingling in a hand in the days after the crash. Neurology and therapy notes tie those symptoms back to the collision. An insurer may argue that a symptom first reported later came from another cause, so a prompt visit and an unbroken record matter.

Gravel, Spilled Loads and Road Defects

Not every motorcycle crash involves contact with another vehicle. A rider can lose traction on loose gravel, fallen cargo or a broken road surface and go down alone. The report may list no other driver, yet someone may still answer for the hazard. Frost Law Group can ask who put the hazard there, because the answer decides which rules and deadlines apply.

A Load That Fell From a Truck

Section 56-5-4100 bars driving on a public highway with a load that can drop, sift, leak or escape. A 2024 amendment carried the older fastening rule into the same statute, so any load and its covering must be fastened securely. A truck hauling rock, gravel or stone must keep the load six inches below the top of each wall or cover it. The statute expressly preserves a civil claim for damages. Some cargo, including forest products and livestock, falls outside those rules.

A Road Hazard a Public Agency Did Not Fix

SCDOT, counties and towns answer for road conditions under South Carolina's Tort Claims Act, and Section 15-78-60(15) covers a hazard that a third party left behind. The agency answers for it only if it had actual or constructive notice and still left the problem in place past a reasonable time. Constructive notice means the agency would have found the problem with reasonable care. Agencies carry no liability under the Act for how a highway was designed. A suit under the Act ordinarily has two years under Section 15-78-110, running from the day the rider found the loss or reasonably should have. That window stretches to three years only when a verified claim went to the agency first. Section 15-78-80(d) adds a deadline for the claim itself, which the agency has to get within one year after that same day. Which deadline controls turns on the facts, and a lawyer should sort it out early.

Helmet Law, Lane Rules, and Fault in a Charleston County Motorcycle Injury Attorney's Case

Section 56-5-3610 gives a motorcycle rider the same rights and duties as the driver of any other vehicle. That section and the rules around it appear in the Legislature's online text of Title 56, Chapter 5. That chapter covers lane use, helmets and the right of way. An insurer defending a driver may search for any rule the rider broke. Frost Law Group can check each of those rules against the evidence before a rider gives a recorded statement.

Most rider claims turn on three questions, about lane position, gear and the split of fault. The answers come from the physical evidence and the medical proof. A lawyer has to review both before offering any view of who bears what share.

Full Lane Use and the Ban on Lane Splitting

Under Section 56-5-3640, no driver may take away a motorcycle's full use of its lane. Lane splitting is illegal under the same section. The term covers riding in the gap between lanes or between side-by-side lines of vehicles. The section also forbids overtaking a vehicle within its own lane and caps riders at two abreast. A car that drifts into a rider's lane on Ben Sawyer Boulevard may break that rule. Scrape marks, crush damage and video can show where the bike sat in the lane.

Helmet Rules and a Rider's Medical Proof

The helmet mandate in Section 56-5-3660 reaches operators and passengers younger than 21. Past that age, riding bareheaded breaks no helmet statute. The helmet sections also say nothing about whether a missing helmet affects a civil injury claim. A helmet protects only the head and does nothing for a wrist, a knee or the skin of a leg. Frost Law Group also handles helmet law disputes for injured riders, starting with the rider's age on the crash date.

Can a Rider Who Shares Some of the Blame Still Recover?

Often, yes. South Carolina's Supreme Court adopted modified comparative negligence in its 1991 decision in Nelson v. Concrete Supply Co. Under that rule, a rider whose fault does not exceed the defendant's can still recover, minus the rider's percentage. Once the rider's share passes 50%, recovery ends. Suppose a jury finds that a rider on Bees Ferry Road was speeding and that the driver who turned across the lane failed to yield. The jury assigns each a percentage from the evidence, and no lawyer can forecast those numbers at the start.

Some crashes involve more than one careless party, such as a truck that spilled gravel and a driver who cut the rider off. A defendant the jury places below 50% of the fault has usually owed only its own share of the damages since July 1, 2005, under Section 15-38-15. One whose share reaches 50% can still owe everything the jury awards. Act No. 42 of 2025 left that line in place. For claims that arose or accrued after January 1, 2026, it changed whose fault gets counted. A defendant in such a case may ask the jury to weigh the fault of someone who was never sued. With that in mind, Frost Law Group can look for every possible party early.

A Sworn Answer on the Driver's Coverage

Under Section 38-77-250, a rider's attorney can send the insurer a sworn demand with the incident report attached. The statute calls for statutory overnight delivery or a mailing whose receipt the sender can prove. Within 30 days, the insurer must disclose under oath its own name, the insureds and each known policy's limits, or hand over the declarations page. Excess, umbrella and fleet coverage stay exempt. That answer helps Frost Law Group decide which other policies to pursue.

Uninsured Motorist Claims When a Driver Forces a Rider Down

South Carolina requires uninsured motorist coverage, or UM, on every auto policy under Section 38-77-150, and the chapter's definitions include motorcycle policies. UM steps in when the at-fault driver carried no insurance or remains unknown. The version of Section 38-77-170 in force since May 20, 2024, sets three conditions for an unknown-driver claim. First, police must learn of the crash within whatever time is reasonable given the circumstances. Second, the rider's failure to identify the car and driver on the spot cannot come from the rider's own carelessness. Third, the claim needs contact with the unknown vehicle, an affidavit from a witness other than the rider, or a recording tying that vehicle to the crash. The last two routes matter to a rider who swerved to avoid a car on US 17 and never touched it.

Underinsured Motorist Coverage on the Rider's Own Policy

Underinsured motorist coverage, or UIM, fills the gap when a rider's losses run past what the at-fault driver's policy pays. Section 38-77-160 obliges every auto insurer to offer UIM in amounts reaching the policyholder's liability limits, but no rider has to buy it. A policyholder may never have returned the signed offer form within the 30 days allowed. In that event, Section 38-77-350(E) required the insurer to put UM and UIM on the policy at its liability limits. Frost Law Group can ask for that offer form along with the declarations page.

Car Policies in the Rider's Household

A rider's UIM search can reach beyond the motorcycle's policy. In its 1998 Concrete Services opinion, No. 24773, the Supreme Court confined stacking to Class I insureds. Stacking is the combining of UIM from several vehicles. Class I takes in the named insured, that person's spouse, and any relative living under the same roof. A rider living with a parent may qualify under the parent's car policy, depending on its terms. Section 38-77-160 adds a limit when the rider's own vehicle is in the crash, since extra UM or UIM then reaches only as far as the coverage on that vehicle. So the motorcycle's own UIM limit matters.

When a Rider's UIM Claim Reaches Court

Section 38-77-160 also sets procedure for suing an underinsured driver. Those rules give the UIM insurer a chance to take part in the case. They also limit that insurer's control over a settlement with the driver, which shapes when a rider can resolve the claim.

Serving the UIM Carrier With the Pleadings

No UIM action may go forward unless the rider serves the UIM insurer with copies of the pleadings in the suit that establishes liability. That insurer may then appear and defend in the name of the underinsured driver. Service starts a 30-day window for the insurer to appear, and the statute bars putting proof of service into the record.

Settling With the Driver's Insurer at Policy Limits

Sometimes the driver's insurer tenders, or offers, its full liability limits to settle. At that point, the UIM insurer may step in and control the defense for its own benefit. The same section bars any UIM policy clause that requires the insurer's consent before a rider settles with the at-fault party. Frost Law Group can review both policies before a rider signs any release.

Damages Above a Statutory Cap

Section 38-77-160 ties UIM to damages beyond the at-fault driver's limits and also to damages beyond a cap or limit that a statute imposes. Section 15-78-120 caps recovery against a government entity at $300,000 for any one person. Suppose a town maintenance truck turns across a rider's lane. The statute's wording raises the question whether the rider's UIM reaches the loss above that cap. The policy language and the crash facts decide that question, and a lawyer reviews both.

How Long Does an Injured Rider Have to File?

Three years is the ordinary limit for a South Carolina injury suit, set by Section 15-3-530. The period may begin later if the rider could not reasonably have discovered the claim sooner, a rule found in Section 15-3-535. A government defendant brings in the shorter Tort Claims Act deadlines instead. Section 15-3-40 can pause the clock while a rider is under 18, but the pause alone cannot carry the deadline past the rider's 19th birthday. The controlling date turns on the parties and the facts, which Frost Law Group can review at the outset.

A Fatal Crash and the Estate's Personal Representative

If a rider dies, the Wrongful Death Act lets a court-appointed personal representative bring the family's claim. Under Section 15-51-20, the recovery goes first to a surviving spouse and children. With neither, it passes to the parents, and without parents, to the heirs. The rider's own suffering before death travels in a survival action under Section 15-5-90. For the death claim, the three years in Section 15-3-530 start on the day the rider died. Section 15-51-41 requires a judge's approval of any settlement, whether in probate, circuit or federal court.
