Does a Crash Without a Ticket Still Support a Claim?

It can. Section 56-5-6170 tells investigating officers not to presume a traffic violation just because a crash happened. Any charge has to rest on evidence. So a crash can end without a citation, and a civil claim still rests on its own proof. Fault in a negligence case turns on what each driver did, which witnesses, photos and vehicle damage can show. Whether that proof is enough turns on details a lawyer needs to see first.

Crosswalk Rules at Peninsula Intersections

Paint is not what makes a crosswalk. Section 56-5-500 treats the part of an intersection joining the sidewalk lines on each side as a crosswalk, marked or not. Any crossing drawn on the pavement counts too. At a corner with no working signal, Section 56-5-3130 has drivers yield to a walker in the crosswalk on their half of the street. The duty also reaches a walker coming so close from the other half as to be in danger. A Walk signal, under Section 56-5-990, gives the person facing it the right of way over all drivers.

Turning traffic on a green light must also yield to pedestrians lawfully inside an adjacent crosswalk, under Section 56-5-970. Picture a driver turning onto Calhoun Street who strikes a woman crossing on the Walk signal. Frost Law Group also handles pedestrian crash claims in Summerville under these statutes. Bike Lanes and a Safe Operating Distance

A driver may not block a marked bike lane against oncoming bicycle traffic, under Section 56-5-3425. Before entering or crossing that lane, the driver has to yield to any cyclist in it. Section 56-5-3435 adds a standing duty to keep a safe operating distance from any bicycle. No distance in feet appears in the statute, so the facts of each pass decide whether the gap was safe.

A Car Door Opened Into a Rider's Path

Under Section 56-5-3822, nobody may open a car door until doing so is reasonably safe and leaves moving traffic unimpeded. The statute speaks of any person. So it reaches a passenger climbing out of a rideshare as well as a driver. A witness or a storefront camera can show how quickly the door swung into the rider's path.

When Is a Scooter a Moped Under South Carolina Law?

The answer turns on the machine, not the name on the sales tag. Section 56-1-10 defines a moped as a cycle, with or without pedals, on no more than three wheels. A gas motor may not exceed 50 cubic centimeters. An electric motor must be designed for an input of more than 750 watts and no more than 1,500. The statute treats a moped as a motor vehicle, whatever powers it.

Moped Rules That Follow a Scooter Rider Onto the Street

Section 56-5-50 applies the state's traffic chapter to mopeds on public streets, except for two of its articles. A scooter that meets the definition generally follows the same signal, lane and yield rules as a car. Two points stand out for riders in the city, one a rule and one a number.

Red-Light Waits of 120 Seconds

Under Section 56-5-970(C)(5), a moped driver or bicycle rider may go through a steady red light in one situation. The rider must first stop fully for 120 seconds. Even then, the rider must use due care, treat the light like a stop sign and decide it is safe to go. A crash after that wait turns on two facts, the length of the stop and the safety of the move.

What Changes When the City Is the Defendant?

A crash with a city vehicle, such as a police cruiser or a public works truck, puts the claim under the South Carolina Tort Claims Act. A two-year clock in Section 15-78-110 starts when the claimant found the loss or reasonably should have found it. Presenting a verified claim to the city before suing adds a third year. That claim has its own deadline in Section 15-78-80(d), since the city must receive it no later than one year after that discovery date.

Section 15-78-120 sets a $300,000 ceiling on one person's claim and a $600,000 ceiling on the whole occurrence. It also rules out punitive damages. Which deadline governs turns on who owned the vehicle and the dates involved, so a lawyer checks it first.
