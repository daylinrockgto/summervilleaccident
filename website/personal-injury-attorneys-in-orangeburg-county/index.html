Passenger Claims Against the Driver Who Left the Road

A Car Pushed From the Pavement by a Driver Who Kept Going

Sometimes a driver swerves onto the shoulder to dodge a car that crossed the center line and never stopped. Section 38-77-170 controls an uninsured motorist claim when no one learns who the other driver was. The legislature rewrote that section effective May 20, 2024. Notice to police has to come within a reasonable time, judged case by case, since the statute names no fixed number of hours. Physical Contact and Independent Witness Affidavits

Physical contact with the unknown vehicle is the first route, such as a scrape that left its paint on your fender. A witness is the second. That person cannot be your vehicle's owner or driver, and the affidavit must display, in capital letters, the false-statement warning written into subsection (B). If no affidavit can be had, South Carolina's Rule 27 allows the insured to seek a judge's order for the witness's deposition ahead of any suit.

Dashcam and Surveillance Video Under the 2024 Rewrite

The third route arrived with the 2024 rewrite. A recording from a dashcam or any other source now counts if it shows the unknown car caused the harm. Many older write-ups of South Carolina hit-and-run rules leave this option out. Asking nearby stores or homeowners for camera footage early can make the difference in which route is available.

Shoulders, Guardrails and Road Defects on Public Routes

A missing guardrail or a broken shoulder edge can play a part in a run-off-road wreck. When the state, a county or a town maintains the road, the South Carolina Tort Claims Act governs the claim. Recovery tops out at $300,000 for each injured person, with $600,000 as the ceiling for a whole occurrence. The Act shields government agencies from claims attacking a highway's design. Some defects come from a third party, and some involve a missing sign or guardrail. For those, the agency generally answers only after it had notice and a reasonable time to fix the problem. Section 15-78-110 sets a two-year suit deadline, measured from when the harm was discovered or reasonably could have been. A verified claim presented to the agency beforehand can stretch that to three years. Section 15-78-80(d) conditions that longer window on the agency having the claim in hand within one year of the discovery date.

Who Brings a Death Claim for an Orangeburg County Resident?

Under South Carolina's Wrongful Death Act, only the personal representative can bring the claim. That means the estate's executor or administrator, appointed through the probate court. Section 62-3-201 generally sends the estate to the probate court of the county that was the person's legal home at death. For someone who lived in Orangeburg County, the estate usually starts in that county's probate court. The representative sues for the spouse and children first, then for parents, then for heirs, as Section 15-51-20 lays out.

The person's own losses, including any pain the person was conscious of before dying, travel in a companion survival action under Section 15-5-90. Funeral costs may be claimed in only one of the two. For the death claim, Section 15-3-530(6) starts its three-year count at the death itself, which can come later than the crash. Section 15-51-41 also puts every settlement of either claim before a court for approval. Frost Law Group handles wrongful death claims that grow out of fatal crashes. How a family's claim proceeds depends on its facts, and no attorney can promise an outcome.

Choosing Between Orangeburg and Dorchester Venues Under Section 15-7-30

Venue is the county where a lawsuit belongs, and Section 15-7-30 ties it to the type of defendant. For a driver who lives in South Carolina, the suit ordinarily goes to one of two counties. One is where the driver lived when the claim arose, and the other is where most of the negligent conduct occurred. An out-of-state driver may be sued in the county of that conduct or in the county the injured person called home when the claim arose. Filing Deadlines and Court Approval for a Child's Settlement

The child's age on the crash date decides whether that extension adds any time at all. When a child would net more than $25,000 from a settlement, Section 62-5-433 sends approval to a circuit court. Approval comes from the circuit court of the child's home county, or of the county hearing the suit, so an Orangeburg County child's settlement ordinarily goes to the circuit court on Amelia Street.

Tractor-Trailer Claims on Both Interstates

Driving-Hour Limits and Six-Month Log Retention

A property-carrying driver generally needs 10 hours off duty before driving. After that, the driver may drive up to 11 hours inside a 14-hour window. A 30-minute break is due after eight hours behind the wheel. The federal floor for keeping duty-status records is six months, so a request to preserve them belongs near the top of the list.

Post-Crash Drug and Alcohol Testing

Federal rules require a carrier to test a surviving CDL driver after a crash that kills someone. Without a death, testing depends on a citation plus either an injured person treated off-site or a vehicle towed from the scene. The citation must come within 8 hours for the alcohol test and 32 hours for the drug test. Whether a test happened, and when, becomes part of the proof.

Insurance Floors That Depend on the Cargo

The federal minimum for a for-hire interstate carrier of ordinary freight is $750,000 in public liability coverage. Oil and most listed hazardous materials push that minimum to $1,000,000. Some bulk hazardous cargo and explosives require $5,000,000. These amounts are minimums, and a carrier's actual policy can be larger.

Loose Cargo and the 2024 Load Securement Amendment

Section 56-5-4100 forbids moving a vehicle loaded so that cargo can fall, sift, leak or otherwise get loose on a public highway. A 2024 amendment added an express duty to fasten any load and its covering securely. Seed cotton, soybeans, tobacco and forest products are among the cargoes the section exempts. The section also states that it does not bar a claim for damages.

Med Pay Coverage and the No-Subrogation Rule

Personal injury protection, or PIP, is not mandatory in South Carolina, and medical payments coverage is optional. Section 38-77-144 adds a rule many drivers never hear about. Med pay or similar coverage that an insurer sells cannot be assigned, cannot pass to the insurer by subrogation and is not subject to a setoff. Subrogation means an insurer steps into your place to collect what it paid, so a med pay carrier generally cannot take that money back from your injury claim.

Repayment Claims From Medicaid and Medicare

Public coverage that paid for crash treatment generally expects repayment from an injury recovery. Two different laws govern that repayment, one state and one federal. A lawyer who has written notice of Medicaid's rights can be liable to the state for disbursing settlement money without honoring them. Both claims therefore need attention before any settlement check clears, and the amounts can change as the records come in.

SCDHHS Rights When Medicaid Paid the Bills

Section 43-7-420 treats a Medicaid patient as having handed over the right to recover what Medicaid paid, up to that amount. The state Medicaid agency, SCDHHS, holds that right. Section 43-7-430 also lets the agency step into the patient's place for the same amount. When the patient has a lawyer, SCDHHS may reduce its claim by 25%.

Federal Reimbursement When Medicare Paid First

Medicare's payment for injury care is conditional whenever an auto or liability policy is responsible, under the federal Medicare Secondary Payer provision, 42 U.S.C. 1395y(b)(2). Any settlement, judgment or award then owes Medicare back for those payments. The amount turns on the records and the claim. A lawyer can confirm the final figure before money changes hands.

Can Five Years of County Data Prove Fault in One Crash?

No. County tables count reports, not fault in a particular wreck. They point to recurring places and factors that deserve a closer look. Fault still comes from the report, witnesses, vehicle data, video and the rules of the road. South Carolina's modified comparative negligence rule bars recovery for anyone found more than 50% at fault. Short of that line, it cuts an award by the person's own share. Filing deadlines matter as well, and a claim against a public agency runs on its own shorter schedule.
