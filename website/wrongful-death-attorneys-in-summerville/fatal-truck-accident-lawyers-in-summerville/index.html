How Fatal Truck Accident Lawyers in Summerville Look Past the Driver to the Carrier

The SCDPS Traffic Collision Fact Book counted 130 fatal collisions involving a commercial motor vehicle in South Carolina in 2024. That was 15% more than the 113 in 2023, and those 130 crashes took 145 lives. At Frost Law Group, a death case against a trucking company can begin with a question about the company itself. What did it know about this driver and this truck before the crash?

More of those fatal commercial vehicle crashes happened on US primary routes than on interstates, 43 against 36. A crash report records what officers found at the scene. It says little about hiring, testing, repair and dispatch decisions the carrier made long before. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville read the company files beside the report. What those files show differs by case.

Section 15-51-10 asks whether the person who died could have sued had he or she lived. If so, the personal representative may bring a wrongful death claim for the family. That test reaches every party whose wrongful act or neglect caused the death, including a carrier. Picture a loaded box truck that crosses the center line on US 17A, Summerville's Main Street. The driver's employer, the truck's owner and the last shop to work on its steering may each answer. Frost Law Group also brings survival action claims for the estate, which carry the person's own losses before death.

Vicarious Liability for a Driver on the Job

Respondeat superior is the rule that makes an employer pay for harm a worker causes while doing the employer's business. James v. Kelly Trucking Co., Opinion No. 26447, filed March 10, 2008, restates the rule. This liability does not depend on any fault by the employer. It turns on the worker's conduct and on whether the worker was on the job. Leases, dispatch logs and pay records usually answer that question in a truck case.

Direct Negligence Claims Under James v. Kelly Trucking

In James, the defense admitted its tractor-trailer driver was negligent and working when he ran a red light. It then argued that the admission left nothing to try on the hiring claim. The court rejected that argument. An employer that knew, or should have known, of a worker's undue risk to the public can answer for its own negligence. That covers negligent hiring, training, supervision and entrustment. Entrustment means handing a worker a dangerous tool, such as a heavy truck.

The court called that liability direct, not derivative. Two justices dissented. Their opinion noted that most other states bar the separate claim once an employer admits responsibility for its driver. In South Carolina, a carrier's concession about its driver does not end questions about its own hiring and supervision. Frost Law Group can build that part of a death case from the carrier's files.

Driver Qualification Files a Summerville Deadly Truck Crash Attorney Demands

The driver qualification file is a folder the motor carrier must keep for each driver it employs. Its contents appear in 49 CFR 391.51. Frost Law Group can name that file in a written request to the carrier after a death.

The duty starts one rule earlier. Under 49 CFR 391.11(a), a carrier may not require or permit an unqualified person to drive a commercial motor vehicle. Qualified means, among other things, trained or experienced enough for the vehicle, physically fit and licensed by one state. The federal file rules bind an interstate carrier once a truck's rating hits 10,001 pounds. For a truck that never leaves South Carolina, Section 56-5-4240 sets the line at 26,001 pounds. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville compare that list with the folder item by item. A gap in the file needs an explanation from the carrier. Its weight depends on how it connects to the crash.

Contents Section 391.51 Requires in Every Driver File

The file opens with the driver's employment application. It also holds the road test record or an accepted substitute, driving records and proof of medical fitness. Each item answers a separate question about the person behind the wheel. Two of them carry dates that keep changing while the driver works for the carrier. A lawyer checks each date against the day of the crash.

Driving Records From Each Licensing State

Within 30 days of hiring, the carrier must ask for the driver's record. Every state that licensed the driver in the prior three years gets that request. After that, the carrier must pull a new record at least once a year. A note in the file names who reviewed each record and when. A driver who held a North Carolina license until a recent move to Ladson should have records from both states.

Medical Fitness and the 24-Month Exam

Under 49 CFR 391.41, no one may drive a commercial motor vehicle without a medical examiner's finding of physical fitness. Under 49 CFR 391.45, a driver needs a new exam at least every 24 months. A new exam is also due when an injury or illness has impaired the driver's ability to work. If a driver lost control on I-26 after a known health problem, the medical paperwork becomes part of the review.

Clearinghouse Queries Before a Driver's First Run

Under 49 CFR 382.701, an employer must query the federal Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse before hiring a commercial driver. The query reveals reported violations, such as a verified positive drug test or an alcohol result of 0.04 or higher. The employer must query again at least once a year and keep a record of each query for three years. A driver with a reported violation may not drive until the Clearinghouse shows the return-to-duty steps are done.

How Long Must a Carrier Keep a Fired Driver's File?

The carrier must keep it for three years after the job ends, under 49 CFR 391.51(c). A company that lets a driver go after a deadly crash still holds the file for that period. Some pieces follow a separate clock. Yearly driving records, review notes and medical examiner paperwork can leave the file three years after the carrier made them. Asking for the file early matters, before older annual reviews reach that mark.

Post-Crash Drug and Alcohol Testing Deadlines a Summerville Truck Wreck Death Lawyer Checks

Take a deadly crash at 9 PM at Exit 199, where I-26 meets US 17A. SCDOT's evacuation diagrams place that interchange in Berkeley County. If the trucking employer has not tested its surviving driver for alcohol by 11 PM, it must write down why. At 5 AM, it must give up on the alcohol test and keep that written record. Drug testing stops 32 hours after the crash, with a written reason.

Those deadlines come from 49 CFR 382.303, and 49 CFR 382.103 limits them to jobs that require a commercial driver's license. Frost Law Group can line up the crash time, the test times and the employer's written explanations. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville treat a missed deadline as a question for the carrier, not as proof. What a result means depends on how the collector handled the test and on the other evidence.

A Death Triggers Testing Without Any Citation

Without a death, federal testing turns on a citation to the truck driver for a moving violation, among other conditions. A death removes that condition. The employer must test every surviving driver doing safety-sensitive work on the truck for alcohol and drugs. Under 382.303(e), the employer may treat a driver who does not stay readily available as having refused. A driver may still leave to summon help or get emergency care.

Police Blood and Breath Tests the Carrier Can Use

Section 382.303(g) lets an employer use police breath or blood alcohol tests in place of its own. The officers must have independent legal power to test, and police urine drug tests count the same way. Each test must meet the applicable requirements, and the employer must obtain the results. So a missing company test does not always mean no test exists. Before he practiced law, Jack C. Frost served fourteen years with the Summerville Police Department and the Charleston County Sheriff's Office. He learned there how investigators collect evidence and put it in writing. A lawyer can request the police file alongside the carrier's.

Test Records With Federal Minimums Shorter Than the Filing Deadline

The three-year filing period in Section 15-3-530(6) begins on the date of death. Federal minimums for keeping test records under 49 CFR 382.401 can run out sooner. A negative drug result or an alcohol result under 0.02 need stay on file only one year. Papers on the decision to test sit with the collection records, kept for two years. Positive results, alcohol at 0.02 or higher and refusals carry a five-year minimum. A preservation letter can name all of them.

Does the Claim Survive When the Trucker Was Killed Too?

Section 382.303 speaks only of surviving drivers, so its testing duty does not reach a truck driver who died. The family's claim continues. Under Section 15-51-10, when the wrongdoer dies, the cause of action survives against the wrongdoer's personal representative. The carrier's own hiring, maintenance and dispatch decisions are a separate matter from the driver's estate. A lawyer can search the probate records for an estate opened in the truck driver's name.

Maintenance and Inspection Records in a Summerville Fatal 18 Wheeler Lawyer's Case

Under 49 CFR 396.7, no truck may run in a condition likely to cause an accident or breakdown. A truck found unsafe on the highway may go only to the nearest place where it can safely get repairs. Even that trip must be less hazardous than leaving the truck where it sits. Frost Law Group can measure a carrier's inspection and repair files against that rule after a death.

Imagine a flatbed whose driver noted a brake defect on a daily report. The carrier sends it back out unrepaired, and it fails to stop for traffic on Dorchester Road. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville would ask for four sets of records in that case. Those are the daily reports, any roadside inspection reports, the repair files and the annual inspection. Frost Law Group also handles truck accident claims for survivors of the same collision. Whether a defect caused a death is a question for the physical evidence.

Daily Inspection Reports and the Duty to Repair

Under 49 CFR 396.11, the driver's end-of-day report covers parts such as brakes, steering, lights, tires, coupling devices and wheels. The rule requires a written report only when the driver found or heard of a defect. An empty folder may mean no one flagged a problem. When a report lists a safety defect, the carrier must fix it before anyone drives the truck again. Someone must then note on the report, over a signature, that the carrier fixed the defect or that it needed no repair.

Before the next trip, 49 CFR 396.13 has the driver review the last report and sign it. The report, the repair note and that signature stay on file for only three months. A brake complaint days before a fatal crash, with no repair note beside it, is the gap a lawyer looks for.

Roadside Inspections and Out-of-Service Stickers

Under 49 CFR 396.9(c), an inspector marks a truck out of service if its condition or load makes a crash likely. No carrier may let anyone drive it until the repairs listed in the notice are complete. After a roadside inspection report, the carrier has 15 days to sign the form confirming it corrected the noted violations. It keeps a copy for 12 months. In South Carolina, the State Transport Police Division of SCDPS enforces those federal rules. Its inspection history on the truck is worth requesting.

Repair Files for a Tractor Sold After the Crash

Under 49 CFR 396.3, a carrier keeps inspection, repair and maintenance records for one year where it houses or maintains the truck. After the truck leaves the carrier's hands, the records need only last six more months. A tractor sold, scrapped or returned to a leasing company after a fatal wreck starts that shorter count. A written request can ask the carrier to hold the tractor and trailer too, since the machines are evidence.

Hours of Service Violations a Summerville Truck Wrongful Death Attorney Traces

Who sent the driver back onto the highway with no driving time left? Under 49 CFR 395.3(a), no motor carrier may permit or require a driver to drive past the federal limits. A driver needs 10 straight hours off duty before driving. Driving has to end when 14 consecutive hours have run since the driver came on duty. Within that window, driving time tops out at 11 hours. Frost Law Group handles truck driver fatigue cases under the same rule. In a death case, the question is who set the schedule behind each violation. Exceptions in 49 CFR 395.1 change those limits for some drivers, so any exception the carrier claims needs testing.

In the Highway Patrol's troop list, Troop Nine is the Multi-disciplinary Accident Investigation Team. If that team worked the crash, its investigation file is another record to request and compare with the driver's log. Carriers must keep hours records for six months or longer under 49 CFR 395.8(k), a fraction of the family's three-year filing period. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville ask for logs, dispatch messages and fuel receipts together. Whether a violation contributed to a death is a separate question the full record has to answer.

Carrier Liability Under the Permit or Require Clause

Section 395.3 applies its limits no matter how many carriers use the driver's services. The count covers the driver's time for every carrier, not only the one whose truck crashed. Suppose a dispatcher hands a driver a load for a Ladson warehouse five hours away. The driver has two hours of driving time left that day. That dispatch record points at the company, and a lawyer compares each dispatch time with the hours remaining.

False Logs and Tampered Electronic Logging Devices

Section 395.8(e) bars a driver or a carrier from making a false report about duty status. It also bars either one from jamming or reprogramming an electronic logging device, or ELD, so it no longer records accurately. A carrier that permits or requires someone else to tamper with an ELD breaks the same subsection. Proof of that conduct can bear on whether a company acted recklessly, which a jury decides with the rest of the evidence.

Earlier Out-of-Service Orders for Hours

Under 49 CFR 395.13, an agent of the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, or FMCSA, may order a driver off the road. Driving past the hours limits or lacking current logs can prompt that order. The carrier may not require or permit that driver to drive again until the driver can lawfully do so. It must also report the action it took on the form and send it to FMCSA within 15 days. An earlier order against the same driver may bear on what the company knew before the deadly run.

Punitive Damages and Insurance Limits a Summerville Trucking Death Attorney Weighs

Many families assume punitive damages can only punish the driver who caused the crash. South Carolina looks at each defendant on its own. Under Section 15-32-520(G), a punitive award against several defendants must be specific to each, and each pays only its own award. So the jury sets any punitive award against the carrier separately from any award against the driver.

Section 15-51-40 allows exemplary damages, another name for punitive damages, in a wrongful death case. The statute requires a wrongful act rooted in recklessness, willfulness or malice. Hiring files, test records, repair histories and logs are where that proof usually sits. Fatal truck accident lawyers in Summerville also look at each defendant's insurance, because policy limits matter when a family weighs an offer. No lawyer can promise a punitive award, and the jury and the court decide whether one fits.

Clear and Convincing Proof of Reckless Conduct

Section 15-32-520(D) requires clear and convincing evidence that willful, wanton or reckless conduct caused the harm. That standard asks for more certainty than the more-likely-than-not test in most civil claims. If any defendant facing punitive damages asks, the trial runs in two stages before one jury. In setting an amount, the jury may weigh similar past conduct, the defendant's awareness and any concealment.

The Base Cap in Section 15-32-530

Section 15-32-530(A) generally caps punitive damages at whichever is greater, $500,000 or triple the compensatory damages. The dollar figure moves each year with the Consumer Price Index. Subsection (B) keeps the cap from the jury, so the court applies it after the verdict. The ceiling rises to four times or $2,000,000 if the court finds management knew of or approved dangerous conduct aimed mainly at unreasonable financial gain. It also rises when the conduct could bring a felony conviction.

Exceptions That Remove the Cap

Subsection (C) of Section 15-32-530 lists three findings that remove the ceiling entirely. The trial court makes each finding, and each one attaches to a particular defendant. That matters when a driver and a carrier face the same verdict. Frost Law Group can review the criminal file and any toxicology results for that reason.

Intent to Harm or a Felony Conviction

No cap applies when the defendant intended harm and did harm the claimant. None applies either when the defendant has a felony plea or conviction tied to the same conduct. Felony DUI resulting in death, under Section 56-5-2945, is one such charge.

Impaired Judgment From Alcohol or Drugs

The third finding concerns impairment. Drinking, or drug use beyond a lawful prescription, must have substantially impaired the defendant's judgment. The statute speaks of the defendant who was impaired. Whether it also lifts the cap on an award against the employer is a question for the court. A post-accident test under 382.303 may form part of the proof.

Earlier Wrecks by the Same Driver on the Company's Books

Federal rule 49 CFR 390.15(b) makes a motor carrier hold each entry in its accident register for three years. An entry gives the date, the nearest city or town and state, and the driver's name. It counts the people hurt and killed and flags any hazardous materials spill other than fuel. Any crash report a state or insurer required goes in the register too. An accident, for this rule, means a crash with a death, a tow-away or an injury needing treatment off the scene. A register that shows the same driver in earlier crashes can speak to what the company knew.

Federal Minimums for For-Hire Freight Carriers

The federal schedule in 49 CFR 387.9 sets $750,000 as the least public liability coverage an interstate for-hire freight hauler may carry. That row governs vehicles rated at 10,001 pounds and up. For oil and most listed hazardous materials, the minimum is $1,000,000. It reaches $5,000,000 for some bulk hazardous shipments. Those numbers set a floor, not a cap on a family's recovery. Private carriers moving their own nonhazardous goods and carriers running only inside South Carolina fall outside the $750,000 row. One deadly crash can put a wrongful death claim, a survival claim and other injury claims against the same policy.

Can the Family's Own Auto Policy Pay Too?

It can in some cases. Under Section 38-77-160, auto insurers must offer underinsured motorist coverage, which pays when damages exceed the at-fault party's limits. James itself grew out of that setting. The injured couple settled with the driver's and the trucking company's insurers, then turned to their own underinsured motorist coverage. That insurer defended the case in the defendants' place, as Section 38-77-160 allows. Whether a family's policy pays after a death depends on its language and the facts.
