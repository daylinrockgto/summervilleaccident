How Personal Injury Attorneys in Beaufort County Handle Crashes Without an Interstate

The 2024 Traffic Collision Fact Book logged 3,895 collisions in Beaufort County, and not one occurred on an interstate. Frost Law Group handles crash claims from the surface roads that carried that traffic instead. US primary routes accounted for 1,744 of the collisions, close to 45%. They also held 11 of the county's 23 fatal collisions.

Officers can enter up to five contributing factors on each report. The most frequent entry was Failed to Yield Right of Way, at 1,030, and 5 of those came from fatal collisions. Ran Off Road, which heads the lists in Colleton and Williamsburg counties, missed Beaufort's top five.

For personal injury attorneys in Beaufort County, that points the work toward intersections, turns and driveways. Still, a factor on a report is an officer's entry, not a court's finding of fault.

Left Turns and Yield Signs Under Sections 56-5-2320 and 56-5-2330

A driver turning left must yield under Section 56-5-2320. The duty covers turns at intersections and into driveways, and it runs to oncoming traffic near enough to pose an immediate hazard. Section 56-5-2330 adds a sharper rule for yield signs. Consider a driver who never stops at a yield sign and collides with a car already inside the intersection. Under that section, the crash itself becomes prima facie evidence that the driver failed to yield. In plain terms, it counts as proof unless the driver rebuts it.

Now picture a left turn from Fording Island Road into a shopping center driveway, across an oncoming car's path. South Carolina follows modified comparative negligence, so a jury may still weigh the oncoming driver's speed and attention. Anyone found more than 50% at fault recovers nothing. A person at 50% or less keeps the award, minus his or her own share of the fault. How fault might divide in a real crash depends on the diagram, the photos and the statements.

Distraction Ranked Second Only in Beaufort and Charleston Counties

In 2024, Distracted or Inattention ranked second on Beaufort County reports, with 882 entries. Among the eight counties Frost Law Group covers, only Charleston shares that ranking. Distraction showed up in 12 serious injury collisions in Beaufort County and in none of its fatal crashes.

Act No. 40 of 2025, known as the Hands-Free and Distracted Driving Act, took effect September 1, 2025. It rewrote Section 56-5-3890. The law bars a driver on a public highway from holding or supporting a phone with the body. Reading or typing texts and watching video are also off limits. The fine is $100 the first time, and a repeat within three years costs $200 plus two points on the driving record.

An officer may not seize, search or view a driver's phone over a violation. So proof of phone use often comes from witnesses, video or the driver's own words. Whether such proof exists after a particular crash takes an attorney's review.

Liability Limits and Uninsured Motorist Coverage After a Beaufort Wreck

Fault drives payment in South Carolina. The state uses an at-fault system, meaning whoever caused the crash, backed by that person's liability insurer, owes for the harm. Section 38-77-140 sets the minimum liability limits at 25/50/25. In dollars, a policy must cover $25,000 per injured person, $50,000 when two or more people are hurt and $25,000 of property damage. Section 38-77-150 also makes uninsured motorist coverage part of every policy.

Underinsured motorist coverage works differently. It is optional, although Section 38-77-160 requires insurers to offer it up to the buyer's liability limits. Suppose a minimum-limits driver pulls onto SC 170 from a side road and causes injuries worth more than $25,000. Your own underinsured coverage, if the policy has it, may then fill part of the gap. How much it adds turns on the policy wording.

Who Pays When an Uber or Lyft Driver Crashes on William Hilton Parkway?

Anyone directly involved, each insurer and the officer on the case can learn whether the app was running or a trip was underway.

Driver Waiting for a Ride Request

Sometimes the driver is logged in and available but has not accepted a request. Section 58-23-1630(B) sets the floor for that window. The required primary liability limits are $100,000 per incident and $50,000 per person for death or bodily injury. Add $50,000 for property damage and uninsured motorist coverage at the basic Section 38-77-150 level. If the driver's own policy has lapsed, the rideshare company's coverage must pay from the first dollar.

Trip Accepted or Rider Still Aboard

Coverage jumps once the driver accepts a trip, and it stays higher until the last rider steps out. Section 58-23-1630(C) then requires at least $1,000,000 in primary liability coverage. That figure is a single combined limit for death, bodily injury and property damage, not $1,000,000 per person. Uninsured motorist coverage at the basic statutory level is required during that period as well. In a coverage investigation, the company and the insurers must exchange the driver's app log times for 12 hours on either side of the crash. Those log times can show which tier was in effect and, with it, which insurer pays.

Town and County Vehicles Under the Tort Claims Act

The South Carolina Tort Claims Act governs a crash involving a town truck, a county vehicle or a defect in a public road. Its clock runs shorter. Two years is the general limit in Section 15-78-110, starting when the harm came to light or should have. Filing a verified claim with the government body first can add a third year. That extra year depends on the claim arriving within one year of the same starting point, as Section 15-78-80(d) directs. Recovery tops out at $600,000 per occurrence and $300,000 per person under Section 15-78-120, with no punitive damages.

Road defects add a notice rule. When a third party caused the defect, Section 15-78-60(15) requires proof that the responsible agency had actual or constructive notice, meaning it knew of the defect or should have. The agency must also have let the defect stand past a reasonable time for repair. Under Section 15-78-100(b), the suit goes to circuit court in the county where the act or omission happened. A child's claim against a government body still gets the Section 15-3-40 extension. Whether a government claim fits these rules turns on who owned the vehicle or kept up the road, among other facts a lawyer has to see.

What if the Driver Who Hit You on Sea Island Parkway Kept Going?

Section 56-5-1210 obligates a driver in an injury crash to stop, share identifying information and render reasonable aid. If the driver flees and nobody identifies the car, uninsured motorist coverage may still respond. The conditions appear in Section 38-77-170, which the legislature rewrote effective May 20, 2024. A report to police has to follow within a reasonable time, judged by the circumstances.

You also need one of three things. First, the unknown vehicle actually struck you or your vehicle. Second, an independent eyewitness, someone other than your car's owner or driver, signs an affidavit about the crash. Third, a recording shows the unknown vehicle caused the damage or injury. Apart from those three routes, the claim fails if you were negligent at the time in not finding out who the other driver was. An attorney has to compare those conditions with the policy and the report before saying a claim qualifies.

Falls at Island Rentals, Resorts and Private Homes

Not every island injury happens on a road. South Carolina courts sort people on another's property into four groups, invitees, licensees, trespassers and children. The owner's duty changes from one group to the next. A resort guest or a shop customer generally counts as an invitee. Owners owe invitees reasonable care, plus a warning about hidden dangers they know of or should discover.

A friend staying at a Fripp Island house is a licensee. Frost Law Group sorts out which group a visitor belongs to before anything else, because that answer can decide whether a fall claim moves forward. Toward a licensee, the owner must use reasonable care in carrying on activities and warn of hidden hazards the owner already knows about.

What if a Child Hurt in Beaufort County Is Taken to MUSC in Charleston?

Medical Bills Paid by Medicaid or Medicare After a Beaufort Crash

Medicaid payments for crash care come with a repayment right. Section 43-7-420 deems the patient to have assigned that right to SCDHHS, the agency that runs Medicaid in South Carolina. The agency also holds subrogation rights. Subrogation lets it step into the patient's place to recover what it paid. It may trim its claim by 25% when the patient has a lawyer, and Section 43-7-440 ranks its right ahead of other reimbursement claims.

Federal law, 42 U.S.C. 1395y(b)(2), makes Medicare's crash-related payments conditional. A settlement or judgment has to repay Medicare. A lawyer can confirm each balance in writing before any settlement money goes out.

Filing Deadlines and Act 42 for Beaufort County Claims

Most injury claims get three years under Section 15-3-530. That period starts, per Section 15-3-535, when you knew about the claim or reasonably should have known. Wrongful death actions also carry three years, but the count begins on the date of death. Claims that arose after January 1, 2026, fall under Act No. 42 of 2025.

Section 15-38-15 has capped a defendant under 50% at fault at its own share of the damages since 2005. Act 42 left that line alone and changed the comparison behind it. A jury may weigh the fault of a wrongdoer nobody sued, if the defendant identifies that person within 180 days of the case's start, or later with good cause. The Act also dropped the old exceptions for gross negligence and alcohol. Intentional, reckless, willful or wanton conduct, or conduct tied to illegal drugs, still carries full liability. Which version applies turns on the crash date and the parties, so an attorney should confirm it for your case.

Personal Representatives for Beaufort County Wrongful Death Claims

Hilton Head Premises Appeals That Began in Beaufort County

Two South Carolina appellate decisions that still guide property injury law began as Beaufort County lawsuits. Frost Law Group looks to them when an owner argues that the injured person saw the danger or that nobody warned the owner. Both involved falls on Hilton Head Island, and both opinions show the appeal coming from Beaufort County. Each involved a hazard the owner had a chance to find and fix.

Davenport v. Cotton Hope Plantation and an Unlit Condominium Stairway

A tenant lived at Cotton Hope Plantation, a condominium complex on Hilton Head Island. He had complained by phone more than once that the floodlights at the foot of his stairway were out. One evening he put his foot on what he thought was a step, found only a shadow, and fell. The trial court directed a verdict against him on assumption of risk.

The case reached the South Carolina Supreme Court in 1998. Knowingly facing a danger like that dark stairway does not bar a claim, the Court held, unless the injured person's fault is greater than the defendant's. The decision left signed waivers and other express agreements to accept a risk in place. The Court sent the comparison of fault to a jury.

O'Leary-Payne v. R.R. Hilton Head II and a Pipe in a Shopping Center Sidewalk

In the 2006 case, a store manager was pushing a cart of flattened boxes along a dim sidewalk at dusk. She had stepped out her store's back door at the Hilton Head Factory Stores II shopping center. A metal pipe sticking up from the concrete caught her foot.

The Court of Appeals upheld the trial court's refusal to direct a verdict for the companies that ran the center. The operators should have seen the pipe during an inspection, the court reasoned, so they had constructive notice. That term means a legal inference that substitutes for actual notice. The jury also assigned the manager 40% of the fault and cut her award by that share.

A crash in a resort parking lot never enters the county totals. Neither does a crash that reached SCDMV only on a driver's own FR-309. Those crashes can still support an injury claim.

Section 56-5-1270 covers crashes no officer investigated that caused an injury, a death or property damage of $1,000 or more. In those cases the driver or owner must get Form FR-309 to SCDMV within 15 days. Leaving out verified insurance information can count as prima facie evidence that the vehicle was uninsured, so what each driver filed can matter to the claim.

Helmet and Eye Protection Rules for Riders Under 21

Anyone under 21 who operates or rides a two-wheeled motorized vehicle must wear a helmet under Section 56-5-3660. Operators younger than 21 must also use goggles or a face shield, per Section 56-5-3670. A windscreen that meets state specifications removes that eye protection duty. Riders 21 and older have no statutory helmet duty.

A Rider's Full Lane Under Section 56-5-3640

Section 56-5-3640 gives a motorcycle the right to use a full lane. Other drivers may not squeeze a rider out of it. The same section bans lane splitting, so a rider may not pass between lanes or rows of vehicles. If a driver on Sea Island Parkway drifts over into a rider's lane, that driver breaks the rule. Still, the rider's own conduct counts under comparative negligence.
