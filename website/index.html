Types of Cases Our Summerville Personal Injury Attorneys Represent

An SCDMV collision report, a store incident report, a dog bite report and a probate court order each start a different kind of claim. Frost Law Group handles all of these personal injury cases. A crash claim runs through the traffic code in Title 56 and the auto insurance chapter in Title 38. A fall on a business floor turns on court decisions about what the owner knew and when.

South Carolina compares the injured person's carelessness with everyone else's. Under modified comparative negligence, you may recover if your share of fault is not greater than the defendant's. The award then drops by your share, and once your share passes 50%, you recover nothing.

Under Section 15-38-15, in force since July 1, 2005, a defendant whose fault falls under 50% generally pays just its own portion of the damages. One at 50% or more can still owe the entire award. Act No. 42 of 2025 kept that threshold for claims arising after January 1, 2026, but it changed whose fault goes on the verdict form. Jurors may now give part of the fault to someone outside the lawsuit, the so-called empty chair. The defendant must disclose that person within 180 days after the plaintiff sues, and a judge may accept a later disclosure for good cause. Act 42 also ended the old rule that kept grossly negligent defendants and alcohol-related conduct liable for everything. When the claim arose, not when the suit was filed, decides which version applies. A wreck from December 2025 stays under the older rules even if the lawsuit arrives in 2026.

Some cases cross categories. A child bitten in an apartment courtyard may have a claim against the dog's owner and questions about the landlord's control of that common area. How fault and coverage divide in a given case turns on evidence that a lawyer must first review.

Under Section 38-77-140, no policy may carry less than 25/50/25 in liability coverage. That means $25,000 of bodily injury coverage per person and $50,000 per crash. Slip and Fall Lawyers

A South Carolina store is not automatically responsible for every spill on its floor. In Wintersteen v. Food Lion, a 2001 decision, the state Supreme Court reaffirmed the test. The injured shopper must show the store put the substance there. Otherwise, the shopper must show the store had actual or constructive notice of the hazard and failed to fix it. Constructive notice means the store should have found the hazard, for example during a routine inspection.

Your reason for being on the property also matters. A customer is an invitee, owed reasonable care and a warning about hidden dangers the owner knew or should have known about. A social guest counts as a licensee and gets less protection. An adult trespasser is protected only from willful or wanton harm. Tenants have another source of rights, since Section 27-40-440 requires landlords to keep common areas reasonably safe. Under the 2011 Bass v. Gopal decision, a business also owes customers reasonable steps against foreseeable crime.

Owners generally owe no warning about open and obvious hazards. South Carolina courts still allow a claim when the owner should have anticipated the harm anyway. In one Supreme Court case, a tenant who knew the stairway lights had been out for weeks still got his claim to a jury. A lawyer can ask for a store's inspection logs early, since constructive notice can turn on them. Frost Law Group handles slip, trip and fall claims from stores, restaurants, apartment complexes and other properties around Summerville. Falls on government property follow the South Carolina Tort Claims Act instead. A fall at a public park or playground, for instance, requires proof that the agency knew of the defect through actual notice and left it unrepaired for an unreasonable time. Whether an owner had notice is a question of fact, and the answer changes from one case to the next.

Dog Bite Attorneys in Summerville, South Carolina

South Carolina has no "one free bite" rule for dog owners. Section 47-3-110 makes the owner, or anyone caring for the dog, liable when it bites or attacks. Protection reaches a person bitten in a public spot or while legally present on private land. The owner's own yard counts as private land. South Carolina's Supreme Court calls the statute strict liability, so the victim need not prove carelessness or a known vicious streak.

The statute has limits. It does not protect someone whose provoking or harassing of the dog caused the attack. It does not cover trespassers either. A landlord who does not own the dog is generally not liable under the statute. The exception is a landlord who controlled the premises and took on the dog's care. A bite claim also looks at who was keeping the dog, since the statute reaches caretakers as well as owners. No dog counts as dangerous under state law because of its breed alone. An owner whose dog does qualify as dangerous must register it with local law enforcement. That owner must also show at least $50,000 in liability insurance or a surety bond.

A physician who treats a bite must report it to the county health department by the end of the next working day. If no physician treats it, the bitten adult or a bitten child's parent makes the report. The statute still names DHEC, but the South Carolina Department of Public Health took over those programs on July 1, 2024. Under Section 47-5-100, the health department then orders the owner to quarantine the dog for at least ten days, at home or at a shelter the notice names. Medicaid and Medicare Repayment Claims Before a Settlement Closes

Medical bills rarely end with the hospital. When Medicaid paid for treatment, Section 43-7-420 treats the patient as having assigned the state the right to recover those payments. The department's right outranks most other reimbursement claims. A lawyer who pays out settlement money after written notice of that right can owe the department directly. Medicare pays injury bills on a conditional basis, and federal law requires repayment from a settlement or award. Med pay works differently. If an adjuster asked for a recorded statement, note the date and the adjuster's name. After a rideshare crash, save screenshots of the trip. After a truck crash, write down the company name printed on the cab.

Bring your health insurance, Medicaid or Medicare card as well. Both programs can claim repayment from a recovery, so the attorneys need those details early. Pay stubs help show lost wages, and a repair estimate shows the property loss. An unsigned release or settlement check from an insurer belongs in the stack too. A short written log of pain, missed work and missed activities can document losses that no bill shows.

For a fall or a bite, the store's incident report, the landlord's name or the dog owner's address help just as much. Names and phone numbers of witnesses matter in every kind of case. If something is missing, Frost Law Group can often request it straight from the agency, the hospital or the insurer. If the other driver's insurer has already written to you, bring that letter as well.

Damages South Carolina Law Allows After an Injury

Damages are the money a court can award for harm. Economic damages cover losses with a receipt, such as medical bills, lost pay and future care a physician documents. The cost to repair or replace a damaged car is an economic loss too. Noneconomic damages cover pain, lost enjoyment and other harm without a price tag. Together, economic and noneconomic damages make up compensatory damages, the kind that repay actual losses. South Carolina's statutory cap on noneconomic damages is written for medical malpractice claims, not for car, truck, fall or bite cases. Future losses, such as a surgery a physician expects, need medical support in the record before a jury can consider them.

Punitive damages exist to punish, not to repay. Section 15-32-520 demands clear and convincing proof that the defendant acted willfully, wantonly or recklessly. The cap in Section 15-32-530 is usually the greater of $500,000 or triple the compensatory award, with the dollar figure adjusted yearly for inflation. In some cases the cap rises, and in others, such as a related felony conviction, it disappears. Government agencies never pay punitive damages under the Tort Claims Act. An award of compensatory damages also drops by the injured person's own share of fault.

Frost Law Group can explain each category against your records rather than in the abstract. Which damages a claim can support depends on the injuries, the proof and the coverage, and no lawyer can promise any amount.

Probate Court Approval for Death Claims and Children's Settlements

Some injury cases need a court's approval before any money changes hands. Under Section 15-51-41, every wrongful death or survival settlement needs approval. A probate court, a circuit court or a United States District Court can give it. Only a duly appointed personal representative can agree to that settlement under Section 15-51-42. A personal representative who files suit outside the probate court must notify that court within ten days.

The appointment itself follows Title 62. A family opens the process in the county where the person lived at death. Priority goes first to the person named in a probated will. A surviving spouse who inherits under the will ranks second. Other devisees, the surviving spouse and then other heirs follow in a set order. Nobody under 18 can serve. A creditor can seek appointment only after 45 days. With limited exceptions, no appointment proceeding can begin more than ten years after the death.

Children's claims follow Section 62-5-433. Any settlement that nets a child over $25,000 requires circuit court approval. The request comes as a verified petition stating that the terms serve the child's best interests. The money then goes through a conservator or a probate court protective order. Smaller claims can go to either the circuit court or the probate court. A parent can settle a claim of $2,500 or less without court approval.

Filing Deadlines Reviewed at the First Meeting

Most injury suits must reach court within three years under Section 15-3-530, part of Chapter 3 of Title 15 of the South Carolina Code. Under Section 15-3-535, that clock starts when you knew, or reasonably should have known, about the claim. A wrongful death suit has three years from the date of death. A survival claim follows the ordinary injury deadline. How it lines up with the date of death is a question the attorneys check. Keep the filing deadline in view even while an insurance claim is open.

Claims against a government agency usually have two years under Section 15-78-110 of the Tort Claims Act, running from the date someone discovered the loss, or reasonably should have. Three years apply instead if the claimant files a verified claim with the agency before suing. Section 15-78-80(d) puts a condition on that route, since the verified claim has to arrive within a year of that same discovery date. The same act caps recovery at $600,000 for any one occurrence and $300,000 for any one person. Shorter paperwork deadlines run alongside the filing deadline, such as the 15 days to return an FR-10. A defendant blaming a nonparty under Act 42 has 180 days after the plaintiff sues to disclose that person, absent good cause for more time. A dog bite report to the county health department is due by the end of the next working day.

Children may get extra time. Section 15-3-40 generally stops the clock while a child is under 18. The extension stops one year past the child's 18th birthday, though the ordinary three years may run later for an older teen. Frost Law Group's Summerville personal injury attorneys can identify the controlling date at the consultation. Which deadline applies depends on who was hurt, who caused the harm and when. An attorney confirms it from the documents rather than from memory.
