You stepped back from a shelf to read a price tag. Your foot shot out on a clear puddle nobody had marked, and by the time you sat up, a clerk was already mopping. Retail Slip and Fall Lawyers in Summerville start at that spot on the floor and work backward in time. What was there, where did it come from, and who at the store knew about it before you did?

The South Carolina Supreme Court's decision in Wintersteen v. Food Lion, Inc. sets the test a store fall claim has to meet. You must prove the store created the hazard. Failing that, you must show it had actual or constructive notice of the danger and still left it in place. Actual notice means someone at the store knew. Constructive notice means the store should have known, often because the hazard sat long enough that an employee should have spotted it. Write down the aisle and the time today.

What Retail Slip and Fall Lawyers in Summerville Look for in a Store Aisle

Start with the square foot of floor where your shoe lost its grip, then widen the view. Frost Law Group reviews a store fall by following a shopper's path, from the front doors past the displays to the registers. Each area raises the same two questions in a different form. Did the store put the hazard there, and if not, did it know or should it have known in time to clean it up?

Wintersteen also describes the baseline duty a store owes. A merchant is not an insurer of its customers' safety, but it must keep its aisles and passageways reasonably safe. Each department of a store tests that duty in its own way.

Hazards a Store Employee Put on the Floor

The first Wintersteen route skips the question of time. It applies when a specific act by the store created the dangerous condition. Picture a worker restocking a cereal aisle who cuts the plastic banding off a case and leaves the loops on the tile. If your foot catches in it, the store's own act may have made the hazard, and the time it lay there matters far less. Proof tends to come from the stocking schedule, the cart the worker used and video of the aisle.

Spills Another Shopper Left Behind

A customer drops a jar of salsa, a toddler tips a cup, or a cracked detergent bottle drips down the aisle. Wintersteen observes that a spilled substance "generally arrives there through the handling of a third party." In that situation, liability requires notice, meaning the staff knew or should have known about the spill and still failed to clean it up. Frost Law Group builds much of a retail claim on this route, since the proof of notice tends to sit in the store's own video and records.

When the Hazard Falls From a Shelf

A shelf can put a hazard on the floor in more than one way. A cracked jar of pickles can drip for an hour, or a box knocked loose can land in the walkway. Once either one reaches the floor, the same notice questions apply as for any other spill. Merchandise that falls onto a shopper, rather than onto the floor, turns largely on how the display was built and what the store knew about it. The Garvin v. Bi-Lo claim over canned goods in Moncks Corner shows how much proof that takes, and Frost Law Group handles those injuries as premises liability claims.

Do You Have to Buy Something to Be Owed Reasonable Care?

No purchase is required. Sims v. Giles, a Court of Appeals case, quotes the Restatement's own example of a business visitor. It is a person who enters a shop to make a purchase or to look at goods on display. Sims also recognizes the public invitee, someone invited onto land held open to the public. Either label makes a browsing shopper an invitee, generally owed reasonable care. Your own status depends on why you were in the store, which an attorney confirms before relying on it.

Produce, Freezer, and Spill Zones a Summerville Grocery Store Fall Lawyer Knows

In 1998, the South Carolina Court of Appeals decided a Bi-Lo shopper's claim over a grape in a checkout line. She blamed the vented bags the store used to sell grapes. The court still required proof that the store knew or should have known the grape was on the floor. Wintersteen cited that ruling three years later when it refused to tie store liability to display methods.

Food, ice and water all find their way onto grocery floors, but each arrives differently. The produce stand, the drink station and the freezer aisle each raise a separate notice problem. The source of the hazard decides who had a chance to find it, and how soon.

Grapes, Greens and Loose Produce on the Floor

A grape or a lettuce leaf can land on the tile seconds before you step on it. Without proof of when it fell, constructive notice is hard to build. The Bi-Lo checkout ruling shows that blaming the display will not fill that gap. Wintersteen's first footnote describes Pinckney, a Court of Appeals case about fallen poinsettia leaves. There, the evidence let jurors infer that a store manager saw the leaves falling and that they stayed down until the next periodic sweeping.

Ice Bins, Soda Fountains and Other Self-Service Stations

The Wintersteen case involved a puddle beside a self-service soda and ice station, and the station did not shift the burden onto the store. The same questions apply about how long the puddle sat and who walked past it. If the water came from the machine itself, such as a cracked drip tray, the proof may turn toward who maintained the equipment. Frost Law Group can trace the source through video and witness accounts, and the answer depends on what that evidence actually shows.

Freezer Cases and Coolers That Leak

Condensation, a clogged drain line or a door that no longer seals can leave water on the tile in front of a freezer case. Unlike a shopper's spill, a leak from store equipment can return to the same spot again and again. Wintersteen's first footnote speaks to that kind of repetition directly. The line between a first drip and a known leak often decides a freezer aisle claim.

A Drip That Started Shortly Before You Fell

A brand-new leak works like any other spill, so the question becomes time. The store generally needs a fair chance to find and clean it. Frost Law Group looks for signs the water had been spreading for a while, such as cart tracks, footprints leading away or a dried rim at the edge. Video from the freezer aisle may show when the first drops hit the floor.

A Leak Employees Had Mopped Before

A leak last week does not, by itself, prove the store should have known about today's puddle. Wintersteen says mere recurrence falls short. The Court still allowed that some hazards come back so regularly that they amount to "a continual condition." That pattern, joined with evidence such as employees' knowledge of it, may be enough to put notice before a jury.

Work Orders and Repair Tickets for the Freezer Case

A repair call on a freezer case can leave a work order, an invoice or a technician's service note. A series of those papers describing a leak at the same case can suggest the store knew the unit dripped. Once a claim is underway, a lawyer can request them by case location and by date.

Coworkers Who Saw the Same Puddle

Employees who mopped the same spot on earlier shifts can be the clearest source of recurrence evidence. Their names may appear on shift schedules, and they can testify under oath once a lawsuit begins. A cone that stood near the case for days, or a towel tucked under the door, can also suggest that staff already knew.

Entrance Mats and Wet Weather Floors in a Summerville Store Fall Attorney's Case

Picture a thunderstorm opening up over a shopping center on Old Trolley Road just as people stop for groceries after work. Every customer who comes through the doors carries water on shoes, cart wheels and umbrellas. The tile past the front mat can turn slick before anyone thinks to reach for a mop.

Rain alone does not make a store liable, and it does not automatically excuse one either. Frost Law Group looks at what the store did once the weather turned, starting with the mats and signs at the door. The two Wintersteen routes apply at the entrance just as they do in the aisles.

Curled, Sliding or Short Mats at the Door

A mat is something the store chose and put down, so trouble with the mat itself can fall under the created-condition route. A corner that curls up, a mat that slides on wet tile, or one too short to catch the water before the aisle begins may each qualify. Photos of the mat's edges and its position relative to the doors are worth taking before anyone straightens it.

Rainwater Other Customers Carried Through the Doors

Water on shoes and umbrellas comes from other shoppers, so notice usually controls this part of the case. Useful facts include how long it had been raining, how many people had come in and whether any employee checked the entrance after the storm began. A manager's instruction to put out cones can show the store knew the floor was wet. Another customer's slip at the same door earlier that afternoon can point the same way.

Is a Wet Entrance Floor an Open and Obvious Hazard?

A store may argue that anyone could see the floor was wet. South Carolina courts apply Restatement (Second) Section 343A, so an owner generally owes no warning about a danger visitors know about or can plainly see. The exception applies when the owner should anticipate the harm anyway, for example because shoppers are likely to be distracted. A sale display just inside the doors can pull a shopper's eyes away from the floor.

Seeing the water does not end the claim either. Under South Carolina's comparative fault rule, a shopper whose negligence does not exceed the store's can still recover, though the award shrinks by the shopper's share. Past 50%, the claim fails entirely. No attorney can say how a jury would split fault in your fall without first studying the video and the store's records.

Sidewalks and Parking Areas Outside the Store

A fall on the walkway outside raises a question that never comes up in an aisle. Who controls this strip of concrete? In a shopping center, the answer may be the store, the center's owner or a separate management company, and the lease often decides it. Frost Law Group can ask for that lease, and any management contract, before deciding whom a claim should name.

Leases That Give the Center Owner the Walkways

In O'Leary-Payne, the store leased its space in a Hilton Head Island shopping center. The lease made the center's owner responsible for managing and maintaining the common areas, including the sidewalks. The Court of Appeals quoted an older rule for owners who keep control of the parking area and sidewalks. Such an owner must use ordinary care to keep those walkways reasonably safe for customers. Leases shape apartment injury claims too, which Frost Law Group handles under a separate statute, the Residential Landlord and Tenant Act.

Management Companies That Inspect the Common Areas

The same case involved a second company. A management firm ran the center, and both it and the owner inspected the sidewalks for debris and hazards. Both were defendants, and the court treated them together. When more than one business inspected or maintained a walkway, Frost Law Group looks at the part each one played in the hazard.

Inspection Logs and Video a Summerville Retail Injury Lawyer Requests

What did the floor look like ten minutes before you fell, and who walked past it? Records a store keeps for its own purposes can answer both questions. In O'Leary-Payne, the owner's own rules told employees to check every sidewalk on a regular schedule. Because one of those checks should have turned up a metal grounding rod jutting up from a sidewalk, the Court of Appeals held that the owner had constructive notice.

Frost Law Group can ask for a store's floor inspection rules and for the records showing whether anyone followed them that day. Tara L. Frost came to law after roughly 16 years in hospitality. Gaps in a sweep log or a shift schedule can matter to retail slip and fall lawyers in Summerville as much as the entries do.

Sweep Sheets and Aisle Check Records

A store may keep a sheet where an employee initials each aisle, entrance or restroom after walking it. Those initials can help or hurt a claim. A log showing a check a few minutes before your fall can weaken a notice argument built on time. A skipped round or a blank hour can support one. Set beside the video and the shift schedule, the log tells more than the initials alone.

Security Video of the Aisle and the Entrance

Store cameras can show when a spill appeared, who caused it and how many employees passed it before you arrived. They can also show your pace, your shoes and where you were looking. Video systems can record over older footage, so a written request to preserve it matters early. Frost Law Group can send one that names the cameras, the date and a window of time before and after the fall.

What Happens if the Store Does Not Keep the Video?

South Carolina law offers a remedy when a party fails to preserve material evidence. In Stokes v. Spartanburg Regional Medical Center, the Court of Appeals ordered a new trial after the judge refused an instruction on missing evidence. The 2006 opinion said the requested wording reflects South Carolina law. Under it, jurors decide whether the party's explanation for the loss is satisfactory. If it is not, they may, but need not, infer the evidence would have been unfavorable to that party. Whether a judge gives that instruction in a store case depends on the evidence and the explanation offered.

Store Records a Lawsuit Can Reach

Before a lawsuit, a store decides for itself what to share. Once a suit begins in circuit court, the South Carolina Rules of Civil Procedure give both sides formal ways to gather evidence, a process called discovery. Two of those tools fit retail falls closely, and each reaches information a shopper could never get alone. Frost Law Group can use both once a case reaches that stage.

Requests to Produce Records and Enter the Store

Rule 34 lets a party request documents and electronically stored information in the other side's possession, custody or control. That reaches video files, digital sweep logs and maintenance records. The same rule allows a request to enter property the other side controls to inspect, measure, photograph or test it. In a store case, that can mean measuring a mat, photographing a freezer drain or testing the tile when wet.

Questioning the Store's Designated Witness

Rule 30(b)(6) lets a notice for a deposition, meaning sworn questioning before trial, name the corporation itself and describe the topics with reasonable particularity. The company must then designate one or more people to testify about matters known or reasonably available to it. A notice in a retail case might list the floor inspection routine, camera retention settings and the incident report process.

Store Incident Reports and Insurer Calls a Summerville Shopping Injury Attorney Handles

The first written account of a store fall is often the store's own incident report. A manager may fill it out using your words, a coworker's version or both. Jack C. Frost began a fourteen-year law enforcement career at the Summerville Police Department before moving to the Charleston County Sheriff's Office. His detective work there included preparing search and arrest warrants and writing reports. He knows a report can blend what the writer saw with what someone else said.

Frost Law Group can read the store's version with that in mind and check it against your photos and your memory. Store falls are one part of the personal injury work the firm handles from its Linwood Lane office. Calls from the store's insurer tend to follow the report.

What to Ask for Before You Leave the Store

Ask for a copy of the report, or at least its number and the manager's name. If the form asks for your account, stick to what you know, such as the time, the aisle and what you saw on the floor. An adjuster may later quote a guess about the cause or your own speed back to you. Photograph the floor, your shoes and any warning sign, and collect phone numbers from anyone who stopped to help.

Calls From the Store's Insurer or Claims Administrator

An adjuster working for the store's insurer, or for a company that handles its claims, may call you at home. The adjuster may ask to record your account and may ask about your shoes, your phone and where you were looking. Those questions go to comparative fault, the rule that reduces an award by your share of the blame. Frost Law Group can take those calls for you. How fault would divide in your case depends on evidence a lawyer still has to review.

Medical Proof and Repayment Claims After a Store Fall

Medical records connect the fall to the injury, whether the first stop was HCA Healthcare Summerville Hospital at 295 Midland Parkway or Roper St. Francis Berkeley Hospital at 100 Callen Boulevard. When Medicaid covers treatment, Section 43-7-420 gives SCDHHS the patient's right to collect those payments from a liable party. Medicare expects its conditional payments back out of a liability recovery.

The Three-Year Clock and Where a Store Case Is Heard

Section 15-3-530 gives an injured shopper three years to file suit in most cases. Section 15-3-535 starts the count once you knew, or reasonably should have known, that you had a claim. In Dorchester County, the Court of Common Pleas sits at the Dorchester County Courthouse. The address is 5200 East Jim Bilton Boulevard, St. George, SC 29477, in the First Judicial Circuit. Wintersteen itself began in Orangeburg County, which shares that circuit. Summerville also reaches into Berkeley and Charleston counties, both in the Ninth Judicial Circuit. The right county for a suit depends on facts an attorney checks first.

Can a Store Blame the Employee Who Left the Spill?

Act No. 42 changed whose fault a jury weighs, starting with claims that arise after January 1, 2026. A defendant in one of those cases can ask jurors to put part of the fault on a wrongdoer the plaintiff never sued. Lawyers call that tactic an empty chair, meaning an absent party the jury could blame instead. The defendant has to name that wrongdoer within 180 days of the suit's start, unless a judge finds good cause for more time.

Section 15-38-15(H) bars the move when the defendant's liability is "imputed to or based upon" the other person's fault. A store sued over its own clerk's mopping generally cannot hand that share to the clerk. A cleaning contractor or vendor raises a different question that turns on the contract and the facts.
