How Long After a Car Accident Can You Claim Injury

The short answer

How long after a car accident can you claim injury in South Carolina? Most people get a three-year window to sue, set by Section 15-3-530. That period does not shrink because pain started days after the crash. The tighter limits are practical ones, set by your medical records and your insurance policy.

Section 15-3-535 sits among South Carolina's statutes of limitations in Title 15. It starts the injury clock when a person knew, or reasonably should have known, of the claim. For most crash victims that date is the collision itself. A lawyer should check the facts before anyone counts on a later start. Children, families after a death and people hit by a government vehicle follow separate rules.

What Is the Legal Deadline to Claim an Injury

For a lawsuit against the driver who caused the crash, the legal deadline is usually three years, under Section 15-3-530(5). Telling an insurer about an injury is a different act, and it does not stand in for a filed suit. Frost Law Group can check which date your three years run from. The claim for the car itself has its own three years under Section 15-3-530(4), counted from the crash.

Three groups work on different clocks. A child gets extra time for the child's own claim, but Section 15-3-40 ends that extension one year after the 18th birthday. A family's wrongful death case runs three years from the death rather than the crash. Frost Law Group answers statute of limitations questions in each of those situations.

Filing alone may not lock in the date. Under Section 15-3-20(B), the filing date counts as the start of a suit only if someone serves the defendant within 120 days. A driver who was out of state when the claim arose, or who later left South Carolina, can raise deadline questions under Section 15-3-30. A lawyer should check those points well before the three years run out.

Two Years for Claims Against a Town, County or State Agency

When the other vehicle belongs to a town, a county or a state agency, the Tort Claims Act replaces the three-year rule. Section 15-78-110 sets that window at two years. It opens when the injured person discovers the loss, or when a reasonable person would have discovered it.

The window reaches three years only for someone who first files a verified claim with that government body. A verified claim is a sworn, written claim to the agency. Section 15-78-80(d) then requires the agency to receive it within one year of the discovery point that starts the two years. Which clock applies depends on facts a lawyer reviews.

A Signed Release Can End a Claim Before the Deadline

A settlement payment usually comes with a release, a signed paper that ends the claims it names. The wording decides whether it covers only the car or every claim from the crash. Frost Law Group can read a release before you sign it, so a repair payment does not close off an injury claim by accident.

Ask for a copy of any release before a check arrives. A release is a contract, and a signed release can end claims for symptoms that appear later, so read every word first.

Why Does a Gap in Treatment Matter

A gap gives the insurer an opening to argue that something other than the crash caused your pain. That argument goes to causation, meaning proof that the collision, and not some later event, produced the injury. The legal claim survives a gap, but the proof can get harder.

Say a driver rear-ended at a light on Old Trolley Road visits urgent care the next day, then skips follow-up care for a stretch. An adjuster may point to that stretch and ask about yard work, a gym visit or an old back problem. Whether and when to see a doctor is a medical decision. The legal point is narrower, since records made close to the crash are harder to dispute.

Frost Law Group can gather chart notes, pharmacy records and work schedules that explain a pause. An emergency visit to HCA Healthcare Summerville Hospital, or to Roper St. Francis Berkeley Hospital, leaves a dated record of its own.

What an Adjuster Reads in the First Medical Record

The first chart entry after a crash usually shows the visit date, the main complaint and the history the patient gives. When that history names the collision, it ties the visit to it. An adjuster compares the visit date with the crash date and looks for any other cause in between. A clear, accurate history on day one leaves less room for that argument.

Missed Appointments and the Reasons Behind Them

Treatment breaks happen for ordinary reasons, such as childcare, a new shift at work or a wait for the next opening. An insurer may still describe a break as proof the injury healed. Records that show the real reason, such as a rescheduling note or a work calendar, can answer that claim. How much any gap matters depends on the injury and the records, and no one can predict an insurer's response.

What About Injuries That Show Up Days Later

They can still be part of a claim. Neither Section 15-3-530 nor Chapter 77 of Title 38, the state's auto insurance chapter, sets a number of days for reporting pain. Section 15-3-535 speaks of knowing about a cause of action, not of feeling every symptom. In most crashes, a person knows at once that another driver hit them, so a lawyer will usually count from that day.

So how long after a car accident can you claim injury that surfaced days later? The deadline usually stays the same, and the real hurdle is proof. An insurer may accept that you felt fine at the scene yet deny that the crash caused a headache that began later. Frost Law Group can line up the report, the medical dates and any witness accounts so that the sequence reads clearly. Tara and Jack Frost also answer questions about injury assessments after a crash for people still sorting out their symptoms.

When the Collision Report Says No Apparent Injury

South Carolina's crash data sorts people by injury level, running from No Apparent Injury through Suspected Serious Injury and Fatal Injury. An entry of No Apparent Injury reflects what the officer recorded that day, not a doctor's exam. In the 2024 Traffic Collision Fact Book, Dorchester County reports counted 81 people seriously injured and 1,226 with other injuries. Those figures come from reports, so pain that surfaces after the officer leaves may never reach them.

Sending SCDMV Form FR-309 When Pain Starts Later

Picture a low-speed bump on Berlin G. Myers Parkway where both drivers trade information and leave without calling police. Section 56-5-1270 reaches a crash like that if someone turns out to be hurt, or if damage hits $1,000, and no officer investigated. The driver or owner then must send Form FR-309 to SCDMV inside 15 days of the collision. Neck pain the next morning may bring that crash inside the rule, and a lawyer can check whether it does.

Do Insurance Policies Have Their Own Notice Deadlines

Yes, most auto policies carry notice and cooperation terms, but those duties come from the policy's wording, not from a statute. Expect your own policy to ask for prompt word of the crash and for cooperation with the insurer. The exact words decide what prompt means, so the policy itself needs a read. A policy may name a set number of days. That term may bind you as part of the contract, and a lawyer can check how it applies.

The SC Department of Insurance's auto insurance FAQ tells a policyholder who receives a summons to notify the company right away. Frost Law Group can compare your declarations page, which lists each coverage and limit, with the notice terms, and it handles claim value questions once the coverage is clear.

Unknown Drivers and the Reasonable Time Rule

One statute does set a notice condition for a specific claim. Under Section 38-77-170, a UM claim against a driver nobody can identify needs a police report. That report must come within a reasonable time, judged by all the circumstances. No hour count appears in the text.

The claim also needs physical contact, a witness or a recording of the crash. On top of that, the injured person must not have been careless about identifying the other car at the time. A late injury does not change those conditions, so the report question comes first when the other driver fled.

Serving the Uninsured Motorist Carrier When You Sue

Section 38-77-150 adds a step that sits apart from any policy notice term. A UM claim cannot go forward unless the injured person serves the uninsured motorist carrier with the suit papers from the case against the other driver. That carrier gets 30 days to appear, with the right to defend in the uninsured driver's name. Policy notice terms still apply alongside that step, so a lawyer reads the policy and the statute together.
