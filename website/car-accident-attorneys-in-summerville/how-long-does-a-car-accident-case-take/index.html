How Long Does a Car Accident Case Take

Get a free case review(843) 419-6653

How long does a car accident case take? No reliable answer comes as a number of weeks or months. Each case moves at the pace of its medical treatment, its insurance coverage and any lawsuit someone files. What South Carolina law does fix are certain deadlines along the way, and those shape the order of every step.

The outer limit comes from Section 15-3-530 of South Carolina's Code of Laws. It gives most injury claims three years to reach court. Section 15-3-20 treats a suit as started on its filing date only if the plaintiff serves the defendant within 120 days. Insurance statutes add shorter marks, such as a 30-day limit on disclosing policy limits.

What Happens Before a Demand Is Sent

Treatment, records and a coverage check come first, because a demand has to describe losses that are already known. A demand is a written package asking the insurer to pay a stated amount. It usually includes medical records, itemized bills, proof of lost pay and photos of the damage. Frost Law Group can assemble that package once the medical picture is clear enough to describe.

The medicine sets the pace here. Until a treating doctor can say whether an injury is healing or permanent, nobody can price future care with confidence. Once a claim settles, a release usually ends the right to ask for more, so later bills generally stay with the injured person. That is why a careful demand waits for the treatment plan rather than the calendar.

Gathering Records From Every Provider

Each provider keeps its own chart and its own billing file, and the demand needs both. One crash can produce records from an ambulance service, an emergency room and several follow-up offices. A patient might start at HCA Healthcare Summerville Hospital, which has a 24-hour emergency room, then see doctors elsewhere in the tri-county area. Some patients reach MUSC, which the state health department lists as an Adult Level I trauma center.

Every stop adds another request and another response to wait for. Wage proof comes from employers, and a self-employed person may need tax returns to show lost income.

Learning the Other Driver's Coverage Under Section 38-77-250

A lawyer can pin down the other driver's limits before writing the demand. Under Section 38-77-250, a claimant's attorney may send a sworn request by a delivery method the statute lists, enclosing the incident report. The insurer then owes a sworn reply inside 30 days. That reply identifies the insurer and each insured and states the limits, or the insurer can supply the declarations page. Fleet, umbrella and excess policies sit outside that duty.

What Slows a Claim Down

Ongoing treatment, a dispute over fault and a second or third insurer are common reasons a claim slows. The SC Department of Insurance says companies should pay claims in a prompt and reasonable amount of time, and that what counts as prompt varies from claim to claim. Its auto FAQ names special investigations, bad weather, coverage issues and the number of vehicles as things that can slow the process.

A dispute over fault can take real work to untangle, because each side builds its own version of the crash. Picture a sideswipe on Ladson Road near Dorchester Road, where each driver says the other drifted. In that setting, the timeline depends on what the witnesses, photos and vehicle damage show. A saved dashcam file or a store camera clip can help resolve that argument. Frost Law Group can gather that proof, though no lawyer can promise when an insurer will accept it.

Claims That Involve More Than One Insurer

A serious injury can outrun the at-fault driver's policy and bring your own underinsured motorist carrier into the claim. Under Section 38-77-160, once the at-fault insurer offers its liability limits, the underinsured carrier gets the option of stepping in to run the defense itself. An uninsured motorist claim adds a different step. That carrier must receive any lawsuit papers and has 30 days to appear. Each added insurer runs its own review before it pays.

Repayment Rights Held by Medicaid and Medicare

A lawyer asks each program for its figure before settlement money goes out, because Medicaid and Medicare both hold repayment rights. Medicaid's claim runs through SCDHHS, the state Medicaid agency, which may step into the injury case or settle its own claim. Medicare conditions its payments for crash care on reimbursement from a liability recovery. Frost Law Group can show a client how these steps affect car accident case value and the net amount received.

Court Approval for Children and Wrongful Death Settlements

Some settlements are not final until a judge signs off. Take a child whose net share tops $25,000. Section 62-5-433 then calls for a verified petition in circuit court, backed by the petitioner's sworn view that the terms serve the minor's best interests.

The money then goes to a conservator, meaning someone a judge names to handle the child's funds. The other route is a protective order, a probate court order that safeguards the money. A parent acting alone can close only a claim of $2,500 or less, with no court and no conservator.

Section 15-51-41 also puts wrongful death settlements before a judge. The same goes for survival claims, which cover the person's own losses before death.

What Changes If a Lawsuit Is Filed

The claim moves onto a court's schedule. In circuit court, injury suits go to the civil division, which the Judicial Branch calls the Court of Common Pleas. The complaint goes to the clerk of court, the plaintiff must serve the defendant, and any UM or UIM carrier must receive the papers too. Service can take real effort when a driver has moved, which is one reason lawyers avoid filing at the last minute. Frost Law Group can explain how the statute of limitations for car crashes fits around each of those steps.

Dorchester County's courthouse stands at 5200 East Jim Bilton Boulevard, St. George, within the First Judicial Circuit. Filing does not end talks with the insurer, and negotiations can continue while the suit moves forward. It does bring court rules, court dates and sworn testimony into the process.

Which County Hears the Suit Under Section 15-7-30

Section 15-7-30 decides venue, the county where a trial happens. For a defendant who lives in South Carolina, two counties usually qualify. One is the county of the driver's home on the day the claim arose. The other is where most of the careless conduct took place.

With several defendants, Section 15-7-30(B) lets the case go forward in any county that works for one of them. A Berkeley County resident who causes a wreck on Dorchester Road near Old Trolley Road could face suit in either county. The Berkeley County Courthouse sits on California Avenue in Moncks Corner, in the Ninth Judicial Circuit.

Discovery and the 180-Day Nonparty Disclosure

Discovery is the fact-gathering stage of a lawsuit. Each side answers written questions, hands over documents and sits for depositions, which are sworn question-and-answer sessions outside the courtroom.

Act No. 42 of 2025 brought in a disclosure step. It reaches claims that arose after the first day of 2026. A defendant who blames an outsider gets a 180-day window, opening when the suit begins, to name that outsider. A judge may allow a later disclosure for good cause. The plaintiff can respond by bringing that person into the case.

Does Every Case Go to Trial

No. Many civil cases go to mediation, and a case can settle at any point before a verdict. Trial remains the backstop when the two sides cannot agree on fault or damages.

How long does a car accident case take once it heads for trial? The court's calendar controls that date. The Judicial Branch's page on South Carolina circuit courts says 49 circuit judges serve the state's 16 judicial circuits on a rotating basis. Court Administration recommends the terms and assignments, and the Chief Justice sets them. Frost Law Group can prepare a case with that possibility in view, though no one can promise a trial date.

What Happens at Mediation

Mediation is a meeting where a neutral person, the mediator, helps both sides look for a settlement. The mediator does not decide who is right and cannot force an agreement. Offers usually pass back and forth until the parties reach a number or stop. If talks fail, the case simply continues toward trial. Anything agreed there still needs the client's approval.

Who Decides Whether to Settle or Try the Case

The client does. Rule 1.2 of South Carolina's Rules of Professional Conduct requires a lawyer to abide by a client's decision on whether to make or accept a settlement offer. A lawyer explains the risks of each path, including how a jury might see the evidence differently than an adjuster. The final yes or no still belongs to the injured person, even late in a case.

Two Trial Stages When Punitive Damages Are Sought

A claim for punitive damages can split a trial in two. Under Section 15-32-520, any defendant facing that claim may ask for a bifurcated trial, heard by one jury in two parts. Part one settles fault and compensatory damages, and evidence that bears only on punishment stays out. Part two happens only if part one produced compensatory or nominal damages. At that point the injured person carries a heavier burden, clear and convincing proof that the harm resulted from willful, wanton or reckless conduct.
