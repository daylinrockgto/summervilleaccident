The short answer

What to do after a car accident in South Carolina starts before anyone drives away. Stop, check on everyone, and call police right away if the crash injured or killed someone. Trade names, addresses and registration numbers. Get checked by a medical provider that day, and return any insurance form an officer gives you within 15 days.

Most of those duties come from South Carolina's traffic code, which sits in Title 56, Chapter 5. Section 56-5-1210 makes a driver stop and stay when a crash hurts or kills someone. Section 56-5-1230 spells out the information and help drivers must give. The police call falls under Section 56-5-1260. No statute orders a doctor visit. Still, the first chart often becomes the earliest record linking a crash to an injury.

What Does South Carolina Law Require at the Scene of a Crash

South Carolina law requires you to stop at the crash site or the nearest point you can reach. You stay there until you meet the duties in Section 56-5-1230. Frost Law Group represents people hurt in Summerville crashes, and what each driver did in those first minutes often shapes the early evidence. Section 56-5-1210 covers crashes that injure or kill someone. A companion statute, Section 56-5-1220, extends the stop rule to a crash that damages only a vehicle with a driver or attendant.

The information duty is short and specific. Drivers exchange names, addresses and the registration number of each car, and a license comes out on request. Each driver also gives reasonable help to anyone injured. That can mean arranging a ride to a doctor or hospital when someone plainly needs treatment or asks for it. A driver may step away briefly to report the crash, but the statute requires coming back. Leaving an injury crash is a misdemeanor, and it becomes a felony when great bodily injury or death results.

Can You Move Your Car Before an Officer Arrives

Yes, in a crash that damages only vehicles, the law expects you to clear the road when you safely can. Section 56-5-1220(B) covers a drivable car that blocks traffic after that kind of crash. Its driver should make every reasonable effort to get it out of the travel lanes. The statute adds that moving a car does not, by itself, make that driver liable or at fault. Picture two cars touching bumpers in evening traffic on Dorchester Road near Old Trolley Road. The SCDPS Fact Book shows officers recorded 53 collisions at that intersection in 2024. Pulling both cars onto the shoulder there follows the statute, and the move alone does not put either driver at fault.

When the Vehicles Must Stay Where They Stopped

The rule reverses in a serious crash. Section 56-5-1210(B) keeps every vehicle in place after a wreck involving great bodily injury or a death. Only the investigating officer can authorize moving it. Great bodily injury, in the statute's terms, means an injury carrying a substantial risk of death. It also reaches serious, permanent disfigurement and a lasting loss or impairment of how a body part or organ works. Leaving the cars in place gives the officer a chance to document where each one came to rest.

When Do You Need a Police Report or an FR-309

You must call police at once only when a crash injures or kills someone. Form FR-309 matters only for a crash no officer investigated, and only if it hurt or killed someone or caused what appears to be $1,000 or more in property damage. The $1,000 figure belongs to the FR-309, not to the police call. Section 56-5-1260 sets the police duty, and it contains no property damage trigger at all.

Section 56-5-1270 puts the officer on a deadline after investigating a crash of that size. The officer then has 24 hours after the investigation ends to get a written account to SCDMV. Frost Law Group can help you order a copy of the officer's collision report once SCDMV has processed it. Say a pickup rear-ends you on North Main Street at the I-26 interchange, Exit 199. An officer works the scene, and your part of the paperwork then runs on a separate clock from the officer's.

Who Gets the Call When Someone Is Hurt

Section 56-5-1260 tells the driver to use the quickest means available, spoken or written. A crash inside a town or city goes to that municipality's police department. Beyond municipal limits, the call goes to the county's sheriff or whichever Highway Patrol office is nearest.

Summerville's town limits reach into three counties, Dorchester, Berkeley and Charleston. A Summerville mailing address does not settle the county either. SCDOT's interchange diagram, for one, places I-26 Exit 199 at North Main Street in Berkeley County. When an injured driver is physically unable to report, Section 56-5-1280 puts that job on a passenger who is able.

Two State Forms With 15-Day Deadlines

Two forms carry their own 15-day deadlines, and they start from different days. After an investigated crash, the officer gives every driver or owner a form for verifying liability insurance. In Highway Patrol materials, it goes by FR-10. Its 15-day return period, set by Section 56-9-350, starts on the date the officer handed the form over. If no officer investigated a qualifying crash, SCDMV Form FR-309 goes to SCDMV no later than 15 days after the crash itself. Missing either filing is prima facie evidence, meaning proof that stands unless someone rebuts it, that the vehicle was uninsured.

What Should You Do About Medical Care the Same Day

Get checked by a medical provider the same day, and tell the provider the injury came from a car crash. Some people feel fine at the scene and notice pain later. A same-day visit puts the crash and the first symptoms into one record.

Frost Law Group cannot tell you what treatment you need, but it can explain why that first chart carries weight with an insurer. The firm can also point you toward an auto injury assessment near Summerville. South Carolina sets no statutory cutoff for a first visit. A long gap may still give an adjuster room to question what caused the injury.

Who Pays the First Medical Bills Without PIP Coverage

South Carolina uses a fault-based system. Section 38-77-144 says the state's auto insurance laws do not require personal injury protection, or PIP, and it describes PIP as a kind of no-fault coverage. Without it, early bills often land on your health insurance. Medical payments coverage may help too, if you bought that optional add-on. Your declarations page lists each coverage and limit in your policy. Frost Law Group can go over it with you to see what applies.

Getting the Crash Date Into the First Chart

The first chart is often the earliest document that puts a date on the injury. Say at check-in that a car crash brought you there, and give the day and the hour. A chart that reads "rear-end crash on Bacons Bridge Road this afternoon" ties the visit to the wreck in plain words. That date can later line up with the collision report and with the FR-10 or FR-309. How much weight any single record carries depends on the whole medical file, which a lawyer reviews with you.

Who Should You Call and What Should You Avoid Saying

Call your own insurance company to report the crash, and keep any talk with the other driver's insurer short and factual. Your policy likely requires prompt notice and cooperation. Those duties come from the insurance contract, not a statute. Title 38 holds the state's insurance statutes. Neither its auto insurance chapter nor its claims practices chapter makes an injured person give the at-fault driver's insurer a recorded statement. Frost Law Group can explain what to say when the other driver's adjuster calls and when a written answer may work better.

Part of what to do after a car accident in South Carolina is deciding who hears your account first. Stick to facts you know, such as the time, the place and the direction each car traveled. Avoid guessing about speed, apologizing, or saying you feel fine before a provider has examined you. Picture a crash at a four-way stop off Central Avenue, followed by a call from the other driver's adjuster the next morning. Saying "I'm fine, I probably should have waited" hands that adjuster two statements that may not hold up.

Reporting the Crash Under Your Own Policy

Your own policy may pay part of the loss even when the other driver caused the crash. Under Section 38-77-150, uninsured motorist coverage is a required part of every auto policy. Its bodily injury minimums are $25,000 for a single injured person and $50,000 for a crash that injures two or more people. That coverage matters if the other driver turns out to have no insurance. When you report, give the date, the place, the other driver's details and any report number, then write down the claim number your insurer assigns.

Handling the Adjuster's Next-Morning Call

The other driver's adjuster may call the morning after the crash and ask to tape your account, calling it a routine step. You can decline, offer to answer in writing, or let a lawyer respond. Saying no politely keeps an early guess about your injuries off tape. Once Frost Law Group represents you, its attorneys can field those calls. Your account then reaches the insurer after you have seen a provider and read your policy.
