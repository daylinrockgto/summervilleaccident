Can You Claim a Car Accident Without a Police Report

Get a free case review(843) 419-6653

Can you claim a car accident without a police report? Yes, in most South Carolina crashes. A claim against a known at-fault driver rests on proof, and photos, witnesses, estimates and medical records can supply it. The main exception involves a driver nobody can identify, where your uninsured motorist claim needs a timely police report.

Two statutes explain that answer. Section 38-77-170 of the South Carolina Code covers the unidentified driver. That claim on your own uninsured motorist coverage requires a report to police within a reasonable time. For ordinary claims, Section 56-5-1290 points the other way. It bars crash reports made under the reporting statutes from proving either side's negligence at a trial for damages, even when an officer writes one.

What Takes the Place of a Police Report as Proof

Photos, witness names, the other driver's details, repair estimates, medical records and any video can take the place of a police report as proof. Frost Law Group represents people whose claims rest on exactly that material. Each item answers a question an officer might have answered. Who drove which car, where did each one stop, and when did an injury first appear?

Say a pickup backs into your door in a shopping center lot off Bacons Bridge Road, and both drivers leave after trading numbers. State crash statistics would never show it. SCDPS omits private property crashes from its Fact Book and names parking lots among them, even lots designed for public use. Your claim then depends on your pictures, the other driver's name and insurer, and anyone who saw it happen.

Evidence That Survives the Drive Home

Some proof lasts after both cars leave. Photograph the damage on both vehicles before any repair. Save every text, call log and voicemail from the other driver. Write a dated account of the crash while the details stay sharp.

Businesses and homes near the lot may have cameras aimed at it, though some systems record over older footage. A letter from a lawyer asking the owner to hold that footage may keep it from disappearing.

When the Other Driver Later Denies Being There

Section 56-5-1340 gives SCDMV room to help in that situation. The agency may reveal who took part in a crash when nobody knows who that person is. It may do the same when a person denies being there.

SCDMV can also share, with someone hurt or left with property damage, what a report says about the other driver's insurance. Both disclosures draw on reports SCDMV already holds, including FR-309 forms that drivers file. Frost Law Group can ask SCDMV about those records while building a claim.

When Must You File an FR-309 With SCDMV

An FR-309 is due at SCDMV within 15 days after an injury or fatal crash that no officer investigated. The same deadline applies when the crash left property damage with an apparent value of $1,000 or more. Section 56-5-1270 places that duty on the driver or owner of each vehicle involved. SCDMV Form FR-309 goes to the agency's Financial Responsibility office, PO Box 1498, Blythewood, SC 29016-0040. Frost Law Group also handles the reporting questions that follow fender benders with no injuries, where that $1,000 figure decides whether the form applies.

Picture two cars meeting at a stop sign in Knightsville with no officer called, and one driver needs an X-ray the next day. That injury alone brings the FR-309 duty into play. The form has two halves. Your half covers the crash, the vehicles, the damage, anyone injured and any witnesses. On the reverse, an insurance agent or company representative affirms the policy was in force when the crash happened.

What the FR-309 Asks You to Describe

The narrative box asks how the collision happened and what contributed, such as road conditions, weather and terrain. Other boxes ask for license and vehicle details and an approximate repair cost. They also cover damage to property like a fence, guardrail or mailbox. A victims section asks for each injured person, where that person went and who took them.

Write only what you saw or know firsthand. The FR-309 warns that SCDMV could suspend the owner's registration or driving privileges if the form does not arrive within 15 days.

Why Your Own FR-309 Will Not Prove the Claim

Section 56-5-1340 keeps reports made by the people in a crash confidential. They exist for SCDMV, the Department of Public Safety and other state agencies that use them to prevent accidents. The same section bars those reports from any civil or criminal trial arising from the crash. Its one exception is SCDMV's written confirmation of whether anyone filed a report at all.

So the FR-309 satisfies a legal duty, but proving fault and injury still takes photos, witnesses and records. How far that other proof goes depends on the facts, which a lawyer weighs with you.

When Is a Police Report Required for Coverage

Coverage requires a police report in one common situation. It arises when you claim uninsured motorist benefits because nobody knows who owned or drove the car that caused the crash. Section 38-77-170, rewritten effective May 20, 2024, sets three conditions for that claim.

First, you or someone acting for you must report the crash to an appropriate police agency. That police report must arrive within a reasonable time after the crash, judged by all the circumstances. Second, one of three kinds of proof must connect the unknown car to the harm. Third, your own carelessness cannot be the reason nobody identified the car and driver at the time.

Those three conditions shape any hit and run claim Frost Law Group pursues. The report rule covers property damage as well as injuries. Can you claim a car accident without a police report when the driver fled? Under the statute, not until a report reaches police within a reasonable time.

Say a driver sideswipes your car on Orangeburg Road and speeds off before you catch the plate. Without that report, the statute gives no right to recover under your uninsured motorist coverage, dashcam clip or not. Whether a particular delay counts as reasonable depends on the circumstances, and an insurer may argue the point.

Contact, a Witness or a Recording

The second condition offers three routes. Physical contact between the unknown car and yours is the first. A witness who was neither the owner nor the driver of your car can supply the second.

That witness signs an affidavit, a signed and sworn statement that carries a capitalized warning about false statements. If you cannot get the affidavit, the statute lets you ask a judge for a presuit deposition, sworn testimony taken before anyone files a lawsuit. Civil Procedure Rule 27 governs that step in South Carolina courts.

The third route is a recording showing the unknown car caused the damage or injury. A dashcam, a phone or a store camera can all produce one.

Notice Terms in Your Own Auto Policy

Your policy likely sets its own duty to report a crash and cooperate, and those terms come from the contract rather than a statute. Section 38-77-200 caps what a UM provision may ask of you. Beyond what the statute or the Director of Insurance's form allows, its only permitted demand is establishing that the uninsured driver is legally liable. Nor may the provision keep you from retaining counsel or going to court. Once you file suit, Section 38-77-150 requires serving your uninsured motorist carrier with the pleadings, the complaint and other court papers, and the carrier has 30 days to appear.

Can You Still Report the Crash Later

Yes, you can usually still contact the police agency for the place where the crash happened. The traffic code even anticipates late work. Section 56-5-1270 describes officers who investigate "after the accident by interviewing participants or witnesses." Whether an agency opens an investigation days later is its decision, not yours. Frost Law Group can help you figure out which agency to call and what to bring.

Section 56-5-1260 names where the driver of an injury or death crash gives notice, and the answer turns on place. Inside Summerville's town limits, that notice goes to the Summerville Police Department at 108 North Cedar Street. Outside town and city limits, the driver notifies the county sheriff's office or the Highway Patrol office nearest the crash. The Highway Patrol and the sheriff also work crashes inside the town.

Highway Patrol Troop Six, Post B covers Dorchester and Colleton counties from its patrol office in Walterboro. Post A covers Berkeley and Charleston counties from its Charleston Patrol Office. Once a report exists, Frost Law Group can help you request a copy through SCDMV or the local agency.

When the 15-Day FR-309 Deadline Has Passed

Section 56-5-1270 sets 15 days for the FR-309 and names no grace period. If the driver or owner never files a properly verified report, the statute lets that gap count as prima facie evidence that no insurance covered the car. Prima facie evidence holds until someone rebuts it. A lawyer can check where a late filing leaves your registration and your claim.

One narrow rule helps when the driver was too hurt to file. If that driver does not own the car, Section 56-5-1280 gives the owner five days after learning of the crash to make the written report.

Late Reports and the Deadline to Sue

A late police report does not change the time limit for a lawsuit. Three years is the usual limit under Section 15-3-530, for injury claims and for vehicle damage claims alike. For injury claims only, Section 15-3-535 can push the start date to when a diligent person would have discovered the claim. Which date controls depends on facts a lawyer should check early, before memories and footage fade.
