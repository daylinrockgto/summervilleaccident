Left-Turn Crashes and Section 56-5-2320

Section 56-5-2320 covers a driver turning left at an intersection or into an alley, private road or driveway. That driver must yield to oncoming traffic already in the intersection or close enough to be an immediate hazard. The duty reaches a turn into a shopping center entrance off Dorchester Road, not only turns at a signal. Stop Sign and Yield Sign Collisions on Dorchester County Roads

Section 56-5-2330 tells a driver at a stop sign where to stop. That means the marked stop line or, if there is none, the near side of the crosswalk or the point with a view of approaching traffic. The driver then yields to any vehicle in the intersection or close enough to be an immediate hazard. A yield sign carries a sharper rule.

Suppose a driver rolls past a yield sign without stopping and hits a car in the intersection. The statute treats that collision as prima facie evidence of a failure to yield. Prima facie means the fact is enough, standing alone, to prove the point unless the other side rebuts it. That shortcut applies to yield signs only.

A stop sign case rests on other evidence. Useful proof includes the driver's own statement, a witness who saw the car roll through and the angle of the damage.

How South Carolina Fault Rules Shape a Summerville Car Wreck Attorney's Case

Many drivers believe that any share of the blame ends an injury claim. South Carolina does not follow that rule. The South Carolina Supreme Court stated the modern test in Nelson v. Concrete Supply Co., a 1991 decision. An injured person may recover if his or her negligence is not greater than the defendant's.

Comparative negligence means the jury compares each person's carelessness and assigns each a share. Frost Law Group can prepare fault evidence with a jury's questions in mind. Until August 2025, Tara L. Frost served Dorchester County as a Magistrate Judge. Presiding over cases showed her how evidence, preparation and credibility hold up when fault is disputed.

Nobody can promise how a jury will divide fault. The split in any case depends on proof an attorney has to test.

Sharing Blame Under Modified Comparative Negligence

Modified comparative negligence works this way. The jury sets your total damages and assigns a share of fault to each party. It then reduces your award by your own share. If your share is exactly half, you can still recover a reduced amount.

Once your share passes half, you recover nothing. Suppose a driver glanced at a phone just before a speeding car ran a red light on Bacons Bridge Road. The jury could assign that driver some fault and still hold the speeding driver mostly responsible.

Multi-Car Wrecks After Act 42 Took Effect

Chain-reaction crashes on Dorchester Road can involve three or four drivers. Act No. 42 of 2025 changed parts of how the law divides payment among them. Those changes reach only claims that arise or accrue after January 1, 2026. A wreck from 2025 stays under the old rules even if the lawsuit comes in 2026.

Joint and several liability means the injured person can collect the whole award from any one responsible defendant. Section 15-38-15 has limited that rule since July 1, 2005, and Act 42 kept its 50% line. The Act changed whose fault the jury counts and which kinds of conduct still carry full liability.

The 50% Threshold for Each Defendant

Take a three-car pileup at a Summerville traffic light. In both versions of Section 15-38-15, a driver the jury holds under 50% responsible pays just the portion of the damages matching that driver's fault. The older version compared fault among the defendants and the injured person. On claims that arise after January 1, 2026, the jury also weighs wrongdoers who were never sued.

Act 42 removed the old exceptions for grossly negligent conduct and for conduct involving alcohol. Full liability still reaches conduct that was willful, wanton, reckless or intentional, or that involved illegal drug use, sale or possession. A drunk driver no longer owes the full award on the alcohol ground alone, though a jury may still find recklessness. Which version of the statute governs turns on the crash date and the evidence in the file.

Section 61-2-147 adds a separate rule for businesses licensed to sell alcohol. It applies when a verdict runs against both a licensee and a driver charged with DUI. The licensee is then jointly and severally liable for 50% of the plaintiff's actual damages, and the charge, not a conviction, triggers the rule.

Empty Chair Fault for Drivers Outside the Lawsuit

For those newer claims, a defendant can ask the jury to assign fault to someone outside the case. Lawyers call that the empty chair. The deadline for naming that person is 180 days after the lawsuit starts, and a later disclosure needs good cause. The injured person may then add that person as a defendant. A nonparty stays off the verdict form in some situations, such as when the nonparty is immune from suit or acted willfully, wantonly, recklessly or intentionally.

Once car accident attorneys in Summerville identify every driver, owner and employer in a multi-car wreck, an empty chair argument is less likely to arrive as a surprise. That work belongs at the start of the case.

Can the Insurer Blame My Injuries on Not Wearing a Seat Belt?

Section 56-5-6540(C) addresses this directly. A violation of South Carolina's seat belt law is not negligence per se or contributory negligence. The statute also keeps the violation out of evidence in a civil action.

Negligence per se means carelessness the law presumes from breaking a safety statute. Contributory negligence refers to the injured person's own carelessness. In plain terms, the seat belt violation itself stays out of the evidence in a civil injury case.

If an adjuster raises the point anyway, Frost Law Group can answer with the statute's text. How the rule plays out still depends on the injuries, the vehicle and the rest of the proof.

Insurance Coverage a Car Accident Lawyer in Summerville Can Pursue

Whose policy pays after a Summerville wreck? In an at-fault state, the first source is the liability coverage of the driver who caused the crash. Section 38-77-140 sets the bodily injury floor at $25,000 for one person and $50,000 for one accident. Property damage coverage starts at $25,000, so the minimum reads 25/50/25.

The South Carolina Department of Insurance explains that every driver must also carry uninsured motorist coverage at those same amounts. South Carolina's auto insurance laws mandate no personal injury protection under Section 38-77-144, so the state is not a no-fault state. The list of policies that might apply starts with the at-fault driver's and your own. Which policies respond, and in what order, depends on policy language and facts a lawyer checks against the statutes.

The At-Fault Driver's Liability Policy and Its Limits

A policy at the 25/50/25 minimum pays no more than $25,000 for one injured person, whatever the medical bills total. Section 38-77-250 lets the claimant's attorney send the insurer a sworn request for the policy limits. The request goes with the incident report, by the delivery method the statute requires. The insurer must answer under oath within 30 days or provide the declarations page.

That rule reaches personal auto policies and leaves out fleet, umbrella and excess limits. Frost Law Group also handles rideshare collisions, which follow their own statute. If the other driver was logged into the Uber or Lyft app, South Carolina's Transportation Network Company Act sets higher coverage floors. The floor depends on whether the driver was waiting for a request or had already accepted a ride.

Protection Under Your Own Auto Policy

Your own policy can pay even when the other driver's policy cannot. Uninsured motorist coverage is mandatory in South Carolina, insurers must offer underinsured motorist coverage, and medical payments coverage is optional. Frost Law Group can review your declarations page early, since an uninsured or underinsured motorist claim runs against your own insurer. That page lists each coverage on the policy and its limits.

Uninsured Motorist Claims Against Unknown or Uninsured Drivers

Uninsured motorist coverage, known as UM, pays when the at-fault driver has no insurance or nobody can identify the driver. Section 38-77-150 requires it on every policy at the 25/50/25 minimum. Claims that involve an unknown driver carry extra conditions, and South Carolina rewrote those conditions in 2024.

Physical Contact, a Witness or a Recording

Since May 20, 2024, Section 38-77-170 has offered three ways to support a UM claim against an unknown driver. First, the unknown vehicle made physical contact. Second, someone other than the insured car's owner or driver saw the crash and signs an affidavit. Third, the insured has a recording showing the unknown vehicle caused the harm.

If the insured cannot obtain an affidavit, the statute allows a request for a court-ordered deposition of the witness. Frost Law Group handles hit-and-run claims under the rewritten section. The pre-2024 version offered no recording route at all, while dashcam or store video can now meet this condition by itself.

Timely Police Reports and Efforts to Identify the Driver

The same section requires the insured, or someone on the insured's behalf, to report the crash to police within a reasonable time, given all the circumstances. It sets no fixed number of hours. The insured also cannot have been careless in failing to learn who the other driver was at the time. Both conditions apply even when a recording exists.

Under Section 56-5-1210, a driver in a crash that hurts someone has a duty to stop and render reasonable aid. Leaving becomes a felony when someone suffers great bodily injury or dies.

Underinsured Motorist Coverage and Stacking Between Policies

Underinsured motorist coverage, known as UIM, pays when your damages run past the other driver's liability limits. Section 38-77-160 makes insurers offer it, up to your own liability limits, though buying it is optional. The same section bars a policy term that requires the UIM insurer's consent before you settle with the at-fault driver. The paperwork from when you bought the policy matters here.

Under Section 38-77-350, a signed offer form creates a conclusive presumption that you made an informed, knowing choice about optional coverage. An unreturned form works the other way. Without a signed form back in 30 days, the insurer had to add UM and UIM matching the liability limits.

Stacking means adding UIM coverage from more than one vehicle, and Section 38-77-160 limits the amount. In a 1998 opinion, Concrete Services, Inc. v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., the Supreme Court limited stacking to Class I insureds. Class I insureds are the named insured, a spouse and relatives in the household, while permissive drivers and guests are Class II. Stacking turns on policy facts, so car accident attorneys in Summerville read every declarations page before counting on it.

Optional Medical Payments Coverage

Medical payments coverage, or med pay, is optional in South Carolina. When an insurer sells it, Section 38-77-144 bars any assignment or subrogation of that coverage and any setoff. Subrogation is an insurer's right to recover its payment from the at-fault side, and the statute generally bars it for med pay. A lawyer checks how the rule applies to a given policy.

Section 38-77-260 adds a protection for benefits paid under your own policy. The insurer cannot have you sign a release or a deduction agreement until it delivers a disclosure statement. At least three days must then pass. That pause gives you time to show the papers to a lawyer.

Can the Other Insurer Delay My Car Repairs Until the Injury Claim Ends?

Section 38-77-330 says no. An insurer may not deny or delay a property damage claim because an injury claim from the same crash is pending. When liability and the amount are undisputed, payment is due immediately.

The Director of Insurance may assess 8% interest on payments an insurer delays without need. If you carry collision coverage, which the Department of Insurance lists as optional, your own policy can pay for the physical damage to your car. The vehicle claim can move on its own track, so the injury claim need not hold up your repairs.

Medical Bills, Lost Pay and Other Economic Losses

Economic damages are losses you can add up with a receipt or a pay stub. They can include medical bills, wages you missed and the cost of repairing or replacing your car. When a treating physician documents care you will need later, that future care belongs in the claim too. Proof comes from billing records, employer letters, tax returns and the physician's notes.

Repayment Claims From Medicaid, Medicare and Health Plans

When Medicaid pays for crash care, Section 43-7-420 lets the state recover those payments from the at-fault side. That right runs only up to what Medicaid paid. If the patient has a lawyer, the South Carolina Department of Health and Human Services may cut that claim by 25%.

Vehicle Repairs and the 75% Total Loss Line

For title purposes, Section 56-19-480(G) sets a 75% line for calling a vehicle a total loss. A car crosses it when repair costs, counting parts and reasonable labor, equal or exceed 75% of its fair market value. With an insurer involved, the statute measures that value as of the date immediately before the crash. The rule does not apply to vehicles worth $2,000 or less or to antique vehicles.

Pain and Suffering Damages in Car Wreck Claims

Noneconomic damages cover harm without a price tag, such as physical pain and the ways an injury limits daily life. South Carolina's statutory cap on pain-and-suffering damages sits in the medical malpractice statute, Section 15-32-220. Juries decide these damages from the testimony of the injured person, family members and treating physicians. The amount in any case depends on the injury, the recovery and how well the records document daily limits.

Punitive Damages When a Driver Acted Recklessly

Punitive damages punish conduct rather than repay a loss. Under Section 15-32-520, they require clear and convincing evidence of willful, wanton or reckless conduct. Section 15-32-530 generally caps them at the greater of three times the compensatory damages or $500,000, adjusted annually for inflation. Compensatory damages are the damages that repay actual losses.

The cap falls away when the defendant intended harm and caused it, or has a felony conviction or plea from the same conduct. It also falls away when alcohol, or drugs other than lawfully prescribed ones, substantially impaired the driver's judgment. A jury decides whether to award punitive damages, and the trial court decides whether a cap exception applies. No one can promise them in advance.

Crash Paperwork South Carolina Law Requires

Beyond the FR-10, South Carolina puts two more duties on drivers after an injury crash. The first is an immediate call to police. The second is a written report when no officer investigated.

Calling Police From the Scene Under Section 56-5-1260

Anyone driving in a crash that hurts or kills a person must tell police immediately, by the quickest means available. SCDMV Form FR-309 is the form for that report. Failing to verify insurance on that filing is prima facie evidence that the vehicle had no coverage.

The Three-Year Limit in Section 15-3-530

Section 15-3-530 gives most injury claims from a car wreck three years. Under Section 15-3-535, the period runs from when the injured person knew, or by reasonable diligence should have known, of the claim. A wrongful death action also has three years, but that period starts on the date of death. Nothing in Section 15-3-530 stops the clock while an adjuster reviews a claim.

Claims Against Government Drivers and Road Agencies

The South Carolina Tort Claims Act applies when a government driver caused the crash or a defect in a public road played a part. Section 15-78-110 generally allows two years from when the injured person discovered the loss or should have. Three years apply instead only if a verified claim reaches the responsible agency before the lawsuit. Under Section 15-78-80(d), that verified claim must be in the agency's hands within a year of the discovery date.

Section 15-78-120 caps recovery at $300,000 per person and $600,000 per occurrence and allows no punitive damages. Government entities are not liable for highway design. For a defect that a third party caused, the agency is generally liable only after notice. Even then, liability turns on whether it corrected the problem within a reasonable time.

Extra Time for Injured Children Under Section 15-3-40

When the injured person is under 18, Section 15-3-40 generally keeps the years before 18 from counting against the deadline. A child may have extra time as a result. The extension has a hard cap and cannot push the deadline past one year after the child turns 18.

For an older teenager, the ordinary three-year period may already run past that cap, so the extension adds nothing. The statute speaks to the child's own claim. A settlement that pays a child more than $25,000 net needs approval from the circuit court under Section 62-5-433.
