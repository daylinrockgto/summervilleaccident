Why Personal Injury Attorneys in Charleston County See So Many Serious Injury Crashes

Charleston County led all 46 South Carolina counties in serious injury collisions in 2024, with 241. Greenville County had more crashes overall, 16,478 to Charleston County's 15,802, yet only 207 serious injury collisions. Those rankings come from the 2024 Traffic Collision Fact Book, which the Department of Public Safety compiles from officers' reports. At Frost Law Group, a Charleston County file starts with that severity ranking in mind.

The county held the same first-place rank in 2023. In 2024, the county's crashes left 291 people seriously injured, and its 3,521 injury collisions of every kind also led the state. Statewide, serious injury collisions climbed from 2,041 in 2023 to 2,139 in 2024, even as fatal collisions fell. Charleston County alone accounted for more than one in nine of the 2024 total.

Deaths followed another pattern. Charleston County's 57 fatal collisions ranked fourth, and its rate of 1.303 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles sat under the statewide 1.68. In Charleston County, the crash burden shows up less in deaths per mile and more in survivors carrying serious injuries. SCDPS put the county's 2024 economic loss from collisions at $509,579,000, using a National Safety Council formula.

Seven Scene Findings That Make an Injury Serious on a Crash Report

The Fact Book takes its injury definitions from ANSI D.16, a national manual for classifying traffic collisions. It defines a suspected serious injury, code 3 on the TR-310 report, by seven signs, and any one of them is enough. They include a severe cut that exposes tissue or bleeds heavily, a broken or distorted limb, and a crush injury.

A suspected skull, chest or abdominal injury also counts, as do second-degree or third-degree burns over 10% or more of the body. So do unconsciousness when taken from the scene and paralysis. Each sign describes what an officer can see or suspect within minutes. Some fractures and brain injuries do not show at the roadside. Suppose an officer on Rivers Avenue codes a passenger as a possible injury because she walked to the ambulance. Imaging at the hospital can still find a fractured vertebra that the roadside code never reflected.

Where and When Charleston County's Serious Injury Collisions Happened

The Fact Book splits every county's crashes by road type and by three-hour window. For Charleston County, both tables cut against a common assumption. Serious injury collisions did not gather on the interstates or during the afternoon rush. More of them happened on secondary roads than on any other road type, and more fell between 6 PM and 9 PM than during any other three-hour stretch. That pattern affects where witnesses and cameras are likely to be. Both findings come from the same 241 collisions.

Secondary Roads Outnumbered the Interstates

Secondary routes, the state roads that carry an S number in SCDOT's inventory, had 84 of the county's 241 serious injury collisions. I-26 and I-526 together logged 1,798 collisions but only 14 serious injury collisions. US routes had the most fatal collisions at 21, and SC routes had 17.

The Three Hours After 6 PM

From 6 PM to 9 PM, the county had 54 serious injury collisions, the most of any three-hour block. In that window, 68 people suffered serious injuries. The window from 3 PM to 6 PM had more crashes, 3,983, and the most fatal ones, 13. Late night was lopsided in a different way. Between 9 PM and midnight, 1,005 collisions included 12 fatal ones, and from midnight to 3 AM, 580 collisions included 8.

Agencies That Investigated the County's Serious Injury Crashes

Under Section 56-5-1260, a driver in an injury crash inside a municipality calls that town's police. Elsewhere, the call goes to the sheriff or the closest Highway Patrol office. So the agency on the report depends on where the wreck happened. Five agencies wrote nearly all of the county's serious injury reports in 2024.

North Charleston police investigated 85 serious injury collisions, the highest count of any agency. Charleston police had 61. Deputies from the Charleston County Sheriff's Office had 60, plus 22 of the county's 57 fatal collisions. Mount Pleasant police had 22, and the Highway Patrol had 6. Even the Summerville Police Department worked 21 collisions in the Charleston County part of town, and the Medical University of South Carolina's own police worked 20, one of them a serious injury crash. The official copy of any of those reports comes through SCDMV. The agency says that version carries more detail than a driver's same-day paperwork, and photos and vehicle damage can then test it.

Does the Officer's Injury Code Decide What a Claim Is Worth?

No. The TR-310 is South Carolina's collision report form. Its printed notice says it reflects the officer's knowledge, opinion and belief, with no warranty of factual accuracy. Insurers and juries weigh medical proof, not a box on a form. A serious injury code can still help, because it records how a person looked minutes after the crash. What a claim is worth turns on the records, the available coverage and the fault evidence. A lawyer has to review all three before forming an opinion.

I-26 ranked second with 1,213 collisions, followed by US 52 at 1,140, I-526 at 585 and US 78 at 534. Those five roads matter for severity as well as volume. A road with fewer crashes can still produce more serious injury collisions, as US 52 shows. SCDOT counted 95,300 vehicles a day on US 17 between I-26 and West Coleman Boulevard in 2024. That was the highest estimate on the route anywhere in the county. Between SC 61 and Lockwood Drive, the estimate was 75,700. The road's 12 deaths also led the county, well ahead of the 7 on US 52.

At a US 17 intersection, Section 56-5-2320 puts the burden on the driver turning left. That driver must let oncoming vehicles pass when they are in the intersection or near enough to pose an immediate hazard. Suppose a driver turning left from US 17 into a side street cuts across an oncoming car. The analysis starts with that duty to yield. Comparative fault, the rule that splits blame by percentage among everyone who caused a crash, can still weigh the other driver's speed.

SCDOT's 2024 counts label most of US 52 as Rivers Avenue or Meeting Street. The road had fewer collisions than I-26, 1,140 against 1,213, yet far worse outcomes. Where I-26 Meets I-526

A chain-reaction wreck at an interchange can involve three or four vehicles. Under Section 15-38-15, in force in some form since 2005, a defendant carrying less than half the fault pays only that fraction of the damages. A defendant at the 50% mark or higher can end up paying everything. Act No. 42 of 2025 left that threshold alone. For claims arising or accruing after January 1, 2026, it lets defendants put an absent driver's fault on the verdict form, generally by disclosing that driver within 180 days after suit begins. How a jury apportions fault in a pileup rests on evidence a lawyer builds after the crash, and nobody can forecast it at the scene.

Lane Changes and Following Distance at the Ramps

Two contributing factors fit interchange wrecks closely. Officers listed Followed Too Closely 2,532 times on Charleston County reports in 2024 and Improper Lane Usage or Change 1,668 times. Section 56-5-1930 asks whether a following driver kept a reasonable and prudent gap given speed, traffic and road conditions. The statute names no set distance. In a merge crash near Aviation Avenue, the answer often sits in the damage pattern and the turn signals. Camera footage can also show who held the lane.

Distracted Driving Claims a Charleston County Car Wreck Lawyer Builds

Since September 1, 2025, South Carolina drivers on public highways may not hold or support a phone with any part of the body. That rule sits in Section 56-5-3890. Act No. 40 of 2025, which calls itself the South Carolina Hands-Free and Distracted Driving Act, rewrote the section. It also bars reading, writing or sending texts, emails and app content, and it bars watching video. A first offense brings a $100 fine, and a second within three years brings a $200 fine and two points on the driver's record.

The 2024 crash numbers predate that law. Distracted or Inattention appeared 2,965 times on Charleston County reports that year, and only Failed to Yield Right of Way appeared more. Neither Dorchester nor Berkeley County had distraction among its five most-listed factors. For Frost Law Group, a distraction claim is a proof problem first, since a report entry records an opinion and a jury wants evidence.

What 2,965 Distraction Listings Do and Do Not Show

Officers may enter as many as five contributing factors per report, so the 2,965 figure tallies entries, not crashes. Three entries came from fatal collisions, 28 from serious injury collisions and 561 from other injury collisions. The remaining 2,373 came from crashes with property damage only.

Statewide figures use a different measure. Across South Carolina, officers named distraction as the primary factor in 9,293 collisions. They named "On Cell Phone" as the primary factor in only 92 and "Texting" in 60. Phone use rarely appears on a report as the main cause, so a claim cannot count on the report to prove it. The county entries and the statewide primary counts also cannot be compared as shares of each other.

Proving a Driver Was Looking at a Phone

Distraction cases rarely rest on one piece of evidence. The proof usually comes from the driver's own words, outside witnesses, video and phone records that a subpoena can reach once a suit is filed. The hands-free law shapes where that proof comes from, because it limits what officers may do with a phone. Each source fills a gap the others leave.

What Officers Cannot Do With a Driver's Phone

Section 56-5-3890(E) bars an officer from seizing, searching or viewing a phone because of a hands-free violation. An officer may stop a driver for the offense only after a clear and unobstructed view of unlawful phone use. For the law's first 180 days, officers could issue only warnings. So a hands-free stop by itself will not put phone data in the police file.

Phone Company Records After a Lawsuit Is Filed

Carrier records can show when a phone sent a text or started a call, down to the minute. They usually arrive through a subpoena, a court-issued demand for records, or a court order after suit is filed. The judge's rulings on scope and privacy limit what comes back. Matching a record's time stamp to the crash time on the report can place a phone in use at the moment of impact. Carrier records usually show activity, not whether the driver held the phone, so they are strongest alongside a witness or video.

Witness Accounts, Admissions and Dashcam Video

Suppose a driver on I-526 drifts across the lane line and clips your car near the Rivers Avenue exit. A driver beside you may have seen the phone in the other driver's hand. The at-fault driver may admit looking down, and the officer may write that admission into the narrative. A dashcam or a nearby business camera can show the drift itself.

Hands-Free Exceptions and the Federal Rule for Truck Drivers

The state law leaves some phone use open. A driver may use a phone for maps or a voice call without holding it, and built-in equipment from the vehicle's manufacturer is exempt. Dispatch data sent or received as part of a driver's job also falls outside Section 56-5-3890. Truck drivers face a stricter federal rule. Under 49 CFR 392.82, no driver may use a hand-held mobile phone while driving a commercial motor vehicle, and no carrier may allow or require it. That federal definition of driving covers a truck stopped briefly for traffic or a signal.

Can Your Own Phone Use Reduce a Distracted Driving Claim?

It can. South Carolina follows modified comparative negligence, adopted by the state Supreme Court in Nelson v. Concrete Supply Co. in 1991. A person hurt in a crash can still recover if the jury finds his or her fault no greater than the defendant's. The award then shrinks by that person's share, and a share over 50% wipes it out. Whether a glance at your own screen matters depends on timing and the other proof, and an attorney weighs that before offering an opinion.

Records That Follow a Serious Crash

A serious injury claim usually rests on four sets of records. They are the collision report, the ambulance record, the hospital chart and the follow-up treatment notes. The report shows the scene, and the ambulance record shows the ride. For personal injury attorneys in Charleston County, the hospital and follow-up records carry the diagnosis, the treatment and the recovery. Insurers focus there when they dispute a claim's value.

Injury and Transport Codes on Form TR-310

The TR-310 collision report gives every person an injury code from 0, no apparent injury, to 4, fatal. It records whether the person went to a medical facility and whether EMS, police or someone else took them. For riders of motorcycles and other two-wheel or three-wheel vehicles, it adds a head injury box. It also notes whether a trapped person came out through mechanical extrication.

Emergency Department and Trauma Team Charts

Hospital charts record triage findings, imaging results and any surgery. At a trauma center, the chart may show the trauma team's first assessment in the minutes after arrival. Those early entries often give the clearest link between the crash and the injury, which matters when an insurer later blames a prior condition.

Transfer Records Between Hospitals

A patient stabilized at one emergency department and moved to MUSC or Trident generates two charts, two bills and a transfer record. The transfer note explains why the first hospital sent the patient on. Missing that first chart leaves a gap an insurer may use to question when symptoms began.

Follow-Up Visits and Therapy Notes

After discharge, the story moves to orthopedic visits, therapy sessions and notes from other doctors. Those records show whether a fracture healed, whether a head injury left lasting symptoms and what work restrictions a doctor set. Keeping every appointment card and bill helps, because a missed visit can look like recovery on paper.

What if a Child Was Treated at MUSC's Pediatric Trauma Center?

Because MUSC is the one pediatric Level I center in the state, a child with the most serious crash injuries may end up there. Two legal rules then come into play. Section 15-3-40 may add time for a child to sue, though the extra time never runs past one year after the 18th birthday. Once the child's net share of a settlement passes $25,000, Section 62-5-433 puts the deal before a circuit judge. For a child who lives in Charleston County, that judge usually sits at 100 Broad Street. A smaller settlement still goes through a conservator or a court, unless it is $2,500 or less.

That court is the civil side of the circuit court, which the Judicial Branch calls the state's court of general jurisdiction. Magistrate court tops out at $7,500 in civil claims under Section 22-3-10, so a serious injury claim ordinarily goes to Common Pleas.

Broad Street or the At-Fault Driver's Home County

Section 15-7-30 decides venue, meaning the county that hears the case. It offers two choices when the at-fault driver lives in South Carolina. One is the county where the crash happened. The other is the driver's county of residence at the time of the crash. For a Charleston County wreck, the first choice always points to Broad Street. The second follows the driver home, to Moncks Corner, St. George or any other county where that driver lived. When a case names several defendants, any county proper for one of them will do.

Suppose a driver from Moncks Corner causes a wreck on I-526 in Charleston County. The case could stay at Broad Street or go to Moncks Corner, where the Berkeley County Courthouse sits at 300-B California Avenue. Both courthouses belong to the Ninth Circuit. The choice affects travel for witnesses and the client alike.

Visitors and Carriers From Outside South Carolina

Charleston County's interstates carry drivers and trucks from other states. Section 15-7-30 treats them differently from South Carolina residents. The rules turn on whether the defendant is a person or a company. For a company, they also turn on whether it registered to do business in South Carolina.

Drivers Domiciled in Another State

A driver who lives outside South Carolina can face suit in the county where most of the conduct occurred. Section 15-7-30(D) also allows the county that was the injured person's home at the time. Picture a visitor who rear-ends a car on the Isle of Palms Connector. The injured person could file at Broad Street or in his or her own home county.

Trucking Companies Formed Elsewhere

Under subsection (F), an out-of-state carrier registered in South Carolina can face suit where most of the conduct occurred or where its principal place of business sits. Without that registration, subsection (G) adds the injured person's home county. Property or business activity in a county is not enough, by itself, to establish a principal place of business there. Any venue choice can draw a motion for a change of venue, which Section 15-7-100 governs.

Court Approval When a Crash Takes a Life

A wrongful death or survival settlement from a Charleston County crash needs a judge's approval under Section 15-51-41. The statute lets the probate court, the circuit court or a federal judge sign off. Section 15-51-42 reserves the power to settle for a personal representative the court has duly appointed, so family members do not settle in their own names. When the suit is filed somewhere other than the probate court, the probate court must receive notice within ten days. Frost Law Group can check early whether the probate court has appointed a personal representative, because that step comes before any settlement.

Filing Deadlines for a Charleston County Lawsuit

Section 15-3-530(5) sets the ordinary limit for an injury suit at three years. Section 15-3-535 can delay the start until the person learned of the claim or reasonably could have. A death claim also gets three years, counted from the day the person died. The Tort Claims Act, not the general rule, governs suits against a government entity. Section 15-78-110 allows two years, measured from discovery of the loss, meaning when the claimant knew or reasonably should have known of it. Sending the agency a verified claim first can stretch that to three years. Under Section 15-78-80(d), though, the agency must have that claim in hand within a year of discovery.

Suppose a Charleston County government truck runs a red light on Remount Road. The Tort Claims Act then applies, with its shorter deadline and a $300,000 per-person cap in Section 15-78-120. Which deadline governs a given crash depends on who caused it and what happened next, so the deadline question comes first.

Auto Collisions and the Insurance Behind Them

The minimum liability coverage in Section 38-77-140 reads 25/50/25. Its first number is the per-person bodily injury limit in thousands of dollars. The second covers everyone hurt in one crash, and the third pays for property. Every South Carolina auto policy must also include uninsured motorist coverage under Section 38-77-150. Section 38-77-160 requires only that insurers offer underinsured coverage, and the buyer decides whether to take it. A serious injury can cost far more than a $25,000 limit, so the other driver's coverage is worth checking early.

Section 38-77-250 lets an injured person's attorney send the insurer a sworn request, with the incident report attached, by one of the tracked delivery methods the statute names. From there, the insurer gets 30 days to swear to its policy limits or produce the declarations page. The statute reaches private passenger auto policies, not fleet, umbrella or excess coverage. Frost Law Group can take that step early in auto wreck claims in Charleston County, before anyone discusses a number.
