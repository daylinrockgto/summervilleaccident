Why Truck Accident Lawyers in Charleston County Study the I-26 Interchanges

Of the 661 collisions at Charleston County's five worst intersections in 2024, officers classed 565 as property damage only. Each of those five locations lies along I-26. Frost Law Group can take up a truck claim from any of them by looking first at the location, the lane and the time.

SCDPS ranks those intersections by the route and street names officers record on each report. For a truck crash, the location on the report can show where traffic merged and which lane the truck held. Each one also raises its own rule or record question, from post-crash testing to electronic logs. Those questions are worth answering before anyone asks a carrier for its files.

Timing matters too. Grouped in three-hour blocks, South Carolina's 2024 commercial vehicle collisions peaked from noon to 3 PM, with 686, while all collisions statewide peaked from 3 PM to 6 PM. For a truck, the clock time of a crash means little until it sits beside the hour the driver came on duty. Together they can show how much of the driver's 14-hour daily window under 49 CFR 395.3 had run. The driving log can then show whether a 30-minute break was due.

Tow-Away Truck Crashes Where I-26 Meets US 78

Officers logged 190 collisions at I-26 and US 78 in 2024, more than at any other Charleston County intersection, and 167 of them caused property damage only. That count cannot be pinned to a single exit, because SCDOT's 2024 file shows US 78 meeting I-26 in two places. One of them is Exit 205 at University Boulevard. Even a crash with no injury can require a federal drug and alcohol test of the truck driver.

Under 49 CFR 382.303, a tow-away crash can obligate the carrier to test a driver who needs a commercial license. The rule needs two things, a vehicle towed off with disabling damage and a moving-violation citation for the trucker. The citation must reach the trucker within 8 hours to require an alcohol test and within 32 hours to require a drug test. Whether the carrier owed a test turns on both facts, which a lawyer confirms from the report and the carrier's files.

Passing Room Behind a Truck at Ashley Phosphate Road

The rule applies on roads outside business and residence districts, whenever conditions allow. A truck trailing another truck must leave a gap that a passing vehicle can safely enter. Picture two tractor-trailers running close together past the Ashley Phosphate Road ramp, leaving a passing car nowhere to pull in. The I-26 and I-526 Junction Seen Through a Truck's Electronic Log

Carriers covered by the federal hours rules generally must use electronic logging devices, with narrow exceptions for paper logs.

Under 49 CFR 395.26, the device logs the date, time, location, engine hours and mileage at every duty status change. When the truck has been moving for an hour without a status change, the device adds an automatic entry. Those entries can show the truck's approximate position and how many hours the driver had logged on duty before the crash.

Dump Trucks and Work Trucks That Never Leave South Carolina

Construction and maintenance work accounted for 477 of the commercial vehicles in South Carolina's 2024 collisions. Of those, 24 were in fatal crashes. Dump bodies appeared on 315 vehicles, 10 of them in fatal crashes. Picture a dump truck hauling fill between two job sites off US 52 without ever crossing a state line.

Under Section 56-5-4240, the federal safety rules reach that in-state truck once its weight or weight rating hits 26,001 pounds. Other law and SCDPS regulations can carve out exceptions. A lighter in-state truck is generally exempt. The exceptions are placarded hazardous loads and vehicles designed or used for 16 or more people, driver included. Otherwise, South Carolina traffic statutes and ordinary negligence law set the driver's duties.

Commercial Vehicle Rules a Charleston County 18 Wheeler Lawyer Applies

Under 49 CFR 390.5, federal commercial vehicle rules start at 10,001 pounds for a vehicle used in interstate commerce. Either the actual weight or the weight rating counts, whichever is greater. A tractor-trailer rated at or over that line and hauling freight between states meets the test. Its driver and carrier then face federal rules on hours and markings, and on drug testing and insurance where those rules reach.

Section 56-5-4010(B) puts enforcement of those rules, and of size and weight laws, with SCDPS's State Transport Police Division. Frost Law Group can ask whether its officers inspected the truck or driver after the crash. Whether a violation actually caused the crash is a separate question, and the evidence has to answer it.

Driving Hours Under 49 CFR 395.3 on a Run Into Charleston County

Section 395.3 of the federal motor carrier rules sets the daily limits for drivers of property-carrying trucks. After 10 straight hours off duty, a driver gets a 14-hour window with no more than 11 hours of driving. Once 8 hours of driving pass, the driver needs a 30-minute break from driving. Off-duty time, sleeper berth time and on-duty time away from the wheel all count toward that break.

Weekly caps stop driving at 60 on-duty hours in 7 days, or 70 in 8 days for carriers running daily. Picture a driver who rested from midnight to 10 AM, came on duty and was still driving on I-26 at 1 AM. That driver's 14-hour window closed at midnight, so the last hour of driving broke the rule unless an exception applied. A violation on paper still has to connect to the crash, which a lawyer weighs with the other proof.

The Carrier Name and USDOT Number on Both Sides of the Cab

Under 49 CFR 390.21T, a self-propelled commercial vehicle subject to the federal rules must display its operating carrier's name and USDOT number. The marking must appear on both sides, in contrasting letters readable from 50 feet in daylight. When another company's name also appears, the words "operated by" must precede the operating carrier's name and number. A photo of the cab can identify the carrier before the full collision report is available.

Picture a leased rig on I-26 with a retailer's name painted on the cab and an "operated by" line beneath it. The carrier on that line is ordinarily the company whose logs and insurance matter first. Frost Law Group takes on tractor-trailer wreck claims in which the tractor, trailer and cargo belong to different businesses. The marking is where that sorting begins.

A for-hire interstate carrier of general freight needs at least $750,000 of coverage on vehicles rated from 10,001 pounds. Oil or most listed hazardous materials require $1,000,000, and some bulk hazardous substances require $5,000,000. These amounts set minimums and do not cap what an injured person may recover.

The 30-day limits disclosure in Section 38-77-250 covers only non-fleet private passenger policies, so it generally does not reach a commercial trucking policy. Out of State Truck Drivers in a Charleston County Trucking Accident Attorney's Case

How does a Charleston County lawsuit reach a trucker who lives in another state and went home after the crash? The question is common in South Carolina, where SCDPS counted 42.7% of the commercial drivers in 2024 collisions as out-of-state drivers. South Carolina licenses covered 1,985 of the 3,466 commercial drivers involved.

A license from elsewhere does not change which rules governed the truck. It does change how a lawsuit reaches the driver and the carrier, and where their records sit. Frost Law Group can plan for both before filing, using state and federal rules written for this situation.

Serving a Driver or Carrier Based Outside South Carolina

A lawsuit moves forward only after the plaintiff serves the defendant with the summons and complaint. Service on a defendant in another state can take extra steps. South Carolina law offers one route for drivers, and federal rules add a second route for interstate carriers. Either route can put a Charleston County case in motion without anyone finding the defendant in person.

The SCDMV Director as a Nonresident Driver's Agent

Section 15-9-350 treats a nonresident who drives on South Carolina roads as naming the SCDMV Director to receive court papers. It covers suits arising from a crash here, including one where someone drove for the nonresident or under that person's direction. Picture an out-of-state driver who rear-ends a car at the Aviation Avenue exit and leaves South Carolina that night. Service on the Director can still reach that driver, if the plaintiff follows the statute's steps.

A Signed Return Receipt and the Affidavit of Compliance

Section 15-9-370 sets out the steps. The plaintiff leaves a copy of the process, with a fee, at the Director's office. Notice of that service and a copy of the process then go to the defendant through a mail service that brings back a signed receipt. Afterward, the plaintiff files the return receipt and an affidavit of compliance with the summons and complaint.

A Refused Envelope and Service by Open Mail

A defendant may simply refuse to sign for the envelope. Section 15-9-380 answers that move. The plaintiff keeps the returned envelope and sends the notice and summons by open mail. Filing the envelope and an affidavit of mailing with the clerk of court then gives the service the same force as personal service.

Process Agents Named on a Carrier's Form BOC-3

Federal rules give a second route against an interstate carrier. Under 49 CFR Part 366, a motor carrier must name a process agent for every state it operates in or crosses. The designations go on Form BOC-3, and each agent must live or keep an office in its state. A carrier running trucks through Charleston County on I-26 should therefore have a named agent in South Carolina.

A Carrier Register That Lists Crashes in Other States

Carriers must keep each entry in their accident register for three years after the crash, under 49 CFR 390.15(b). Each entry names the state and nearest town, the date, the driver and the count of injuries and deaths. For an out-of-state carrier, that register can show crashes elsewhere that no South Carolina report mentions.

Frost Law Group can request the register, and discovery offers a route if the carrier will not hand it over. A carrier based elsewhere may keep its files far from Charleston County. What an earlier crash proves about this one is a question the court decides under the rules of evidence.

Federal Court When the Driver and Carrier Come From Other States

Out-of-state defendants can sometimes move a case to federal court. Section 1332 of Title 28 of the United States Code covers suits between citizens of different states. More than $75,000 must be at stake, not counting interest and costs.

Section 1441 of that title lets a defendant remove such a case to the federal division covering the county. For Charleston County, Section 121 names the Charleston Division, which holds court in Charleston. Section 1446 generally gives the defendant 30 days after receiving the complaint to file for removal.

Diversity alone cannot support removal if any properly joined and served defendant is a South Carolina citizen. Suppose a shipper incorporated and based in South Carolina loaded the trailer, and the plaintiff properly joins and serves it. The case can then stay in the Court of Common Pleas at 100 Broad Street. A lawyer sorts out every party's citizenship before filing.

Does an Out-of-State License Change Which Trucking Rules Apply?

No, the federal rules follow the vehicle and the interstate trip, whatever state issued the driver's license. South Carolina's 26,001-pound rule in Section 56-5-4240 matters only for trucks operating entirely inside the state. Its traffic statutes, such as the following-distance rule in Section 56-5-1930, apply to every driver on its roads. The trip records show which set of rules governed the truck.

Treatment Away From the Scene and the Carrier's Testing Duty

The federal testing rule turns partly on medical care. Under 49 CFR 382.303, an injury crash can trigger a drug and alcohol test. Someone must immediately receive treatment away from the scene, and the truck driver must get a timely moving-violation citation.

An ambulance record and an emergency department arrival time can prove the first element. Picture a driver carried from the University Boulevard ramp by ambulance as an officer tickets the trucker. The citation and the carrier's test file would prove the rest, if they exist. Whether a test happened, and what it showed, are facts a lawyer requests in writing.

A Three-Year Filing Deadline Against Short Record Periods

Most South Carolina injury suits have three years under Section 15-3-530. The Tort Claims Act governs a crash with a government truck instead, and its Section 15-78-110 sets two years from when the loss was discovered or reasonably should have been. A third year opens only if a verified claim reached the agency ahead of any suit. Section 15-78-80(d) makes the agency's receipt of that claim due within one year of the same date. Federal record rules for trucking companies run shorter still.

Driver vehicle inspection reports need to survive only three months, and hours-of-service records six months. Once those minimums pass, the federal rules no longer require the carrier to keep either file, even while an injured person is still in treatment. Frost Law Group can put a preservation request in writing before those periods run out.

What if the Truck Driver Also Went to the Hospital?

The testing duty stays in place. Under Section 382.303(e), nothing in the rule delays necessary medical care. A driver may also leave the scene to get emergency treatment. A driver facing a required test still has to stay readily available for it.

Section 382.303(g) accepts a police breath or blood test for alcohol and a police urine test for drugs. Each must meet the governing testing standards, and the carrier must obtain the results. A lawyer can ask the carrier and the investigating agency for both.
