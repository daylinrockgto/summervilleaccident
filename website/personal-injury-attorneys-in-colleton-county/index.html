Lane Changes and Following Distance at Interstate Speed

Underinsured Coverage When the Other Driver's Limits Fall Short

For a vehicle garaged in South Carolina, Section 38-77-140 sets the liability floor at $25,000 for each injured person, $50,000 for all bodily injury in one accident, and $25,000 for damaged property. Those floors can run out after a serious interstate wreck. Insurers must offer underinsured motorist coverage up to the buyer's liability limits, though buying it is optional. Under Section 38-77-160, that coverage can pay damages beyond the at-fault driver's limits. A separate statute, Section 38-77-250, covers the other driver's personal auto policy. On a proper request from the injured person's attorney, that insurer owes a sworn statement of its limits within 30 days.

In Concrete Services v. United States Fidelity and Guaranty Co., a 1998 decision, the state Supreme Court held that only a Class I insured may stack coverage from more than one policy. Class I, as the court used the term, reaches the named insured, that insured's spouse and any relatives residing in the same household. Frost Law Group can ask for the declarations page on every policy that might apply, because stacking turns on policy details.

Who Pays When Several Drivers Share Blame for an I-95 Pileup?

South Carolina divides fault among everyone who caused a wreck. A claimant whose share of blame tops 50% gets nothing, and a smaller share trims the award by that percentage. Section 15-38-15 splits payment among defendants, and since 2005 it has let one with less than half the fault pay just its own slice. Act No. 42 of 2025 kept that line but narrowed its exceptions for claims arising after January 1, 2026. Only reckless, willful, wanton or intentional conduct, or illegal drug conduct, still leaves such a defendant owing everything. The Act also lets defendants point the jury toward a driver outside the case. They generally must name that driver within 180 days after the suit starts. A 2025 pileup still falls under the older version, whose exceptions also covered gross negligence and alcohol. How blame splits in any chain-reaction wreck depends on evidence gathered after the crash.

Proof When the Other Vehicle Never Made Contact

Contact with the unknown vehicle is one path, and a no-contact wreck cannot take it. Two other paths remain. One is a witness, and since May 20, 2024, the other is a recording. Whatever the path, the crash had to reach police within a reasonable time. And the insured's own carelessness cannot be the reason the other driver went unidentified.

An Independent Witness Account Under Oath

A bystander, meaning anyone besides the insured car's owner or driver, can describe the crash in a signed affidavit. The form has to include the statute's capitalized warning about false statements. Some witnesses balk at signing. If a Colleton witness refuses, Section 38-77-170 lets the insured ask a judge for a deposition, sworn questioning recorded outside court. Rule 27 of the state's civil procedure rules allows it before any suit exists.

Video Showing the Unknown Vehicle Caused the Wreck

Since the 2024 rewrite, a recording can fill the role that contact or a witness would otherwise play. It has to show the unidentified vehicle causing the harm. A dashcam clip, a passenger's phone video or a camera at a gas station on US 17 could fit. Some systems overwrite old footage, so a request to the owner should go out right away.

Reporting a No-Contact Crash to Police

No fixed number of hours appears in Section 38-77-170 for reporting an unknown driver. The test is whether the report came promptly enough given everything that happened. A separate statute, Section 56-5-1260, tells drivers in an injury crash to call police at once. Beyond municipal limits, the statute sends that call to the sheriff or the Highway Patrol office closest to the scene. Can a Passenger Recover When the Driver Swerved for a Deer?

Sometimes. The speed rule in Section 56-5-1520 binds the driver of your own car as much as any stranger. Going too fast on a dark stretch of US 21, or yanking the wheel into oncoming traffic, may put fault on the driver. Reacting sensibly to a deer that bolted into the headlights may not. Passengers in that position usually look to the driver's liability coverage, and the evidence decides which version of events holds up.

Road Defect Claims Against a Public Agency

A missing sign, a dark signal or a bent guardrail can add to a run-off-road crash. Suits against the state or a local government follow the South Carolina Tort Claims Act. Under that Act, Section 15-78-60(15) protects public agencies from claims over highway design. An agency answers for a defect someone else created only if it knew or should have known of it and let a reasonable time pass without a repair.

The Act caps recovery against a public body at $300,000 when one person is hurt and $600,000 for all claims from a single occurrence. Maintenance logs and proof of notice decide whether a road condition supports a claim, and both need a lawyer's review first.

Alcohol Estimates and Bar Service Behind Colleton Wrecks

South Carolina lets a person hurt by a drunk driver sue a bar that knowingly served that driver while intoxicated, under Tobias v. Sports Club (1998). Section 61-2-147 changed the picture for claims arising after January 1, 2026. When a verdict goes against both the bar and a driver charged with DUI, the bar is jointly and severally liable for half of the actual damages. Put plainly, the injured person can generally collect that entire half from the bar. Whether a bar claim exists depends on receipts, video and witnesses gathered after the crash.

Claims a Family Can Bring After a Fatal Colleton Wreck

No claim makes up for a death on a Colleton road, but South Carolina law does give the family two related claims. A personal representative brings both. That is the person a probate court appoints to run the estate, and relatives do not sue in their own names. Section 15-3-530 gives the wrongful death claim a three-year clock that begins at the death itself. Frost Law Group can walk a family through how the two claims divide the losses.

Wrongful Death Damages That Go to the Family

Section 15-51-10 permits a suit when a wrongful act, neglect or default caused the death. Any recovery goes first to a spouse and children. With none, it goes to parents, and then to heirs, in the shares a person without a will would leave. Where the conduct was reckless, willful or malicious, Section 15-51-40 allows a jury to award punitive damages too, an extra sum meant to punish the wrongdoer. The usual punitive cap in Section 15-32-530 lifts when alcohol, or drugs taken outside a lawful prescription, left the defendant's judgment substantially impaired.

Survival Damages That Belong to the Estate

Section 15-5-90 lets the injured person's own claim survive death. That survival claim can reach conscious pain and suffering before death. Its recovery belongs to the estate, not to the family members directly. Reasonable funeral costs can go in either claim, but Section 15-5-100 permits them in just one.

Venue When the At-Fault Driver Lives in Another State

Section 15-7-30(D) governs suits against people domiciled outside South Carolina. The injured person may file where most of the negligent conduct happened. The alternative is wherever the injured person was living when the wreck happened. Say a North Carolina driver rear-ends a resident of the Dorchester County side of Summerville at the SC 64 interchange. Colleton County and Dorchester County could both qualify. With more than one defendant, subsection (B) lets the case go wherever venue is proper for at least one. Nothing in the statute turns on where a hospital sits, so treatment at MUSC or Trident does not move a Colleton case to Charleston County. Witnesses, doctors and travel differ for each option, and the answer changes from claim to claim.

Deadlines That Apply to a Colleton Crash

Section 15-3-530 allows three years for most injury suits. The clock begins, under Section 15-3-535, once the person discovered the claim or reasonably should have. Wrongful death actions get three years counted from the death itself. A suit against a public agency runs on the Tort Claims Act's own clock instead. Section 15-78-110 starts a two-year period once the claimant discovers the loss, or reasonably should have. A verified claim delivered to the agency first extends that to three years, provided the agency receives it within one year of that discovery, as Section 15-78-80(d) requires. An injured child may gain extra time under Section 15-3-40, though that extension stops one year after the child turns 18. The facts and the parties decide which limit controls, so deadlines are an early question for personal injury attorneys in Colleton County.

Circuit Court Approval of a Colleton Child's Settlement

Section 62-5-433 adds a step when the injured person is a minor. If the net amount to the child exceeds $25,000, the circuit court must approve the settlement on a verified petition. The statute names the circuit court where the child resides, or the one where the lawsuit is pending. For a child living in Colleton, that usually means Hampton Street in Walterboro. A conservator, whom the court names to handle a minor's property, then receives the money, unless the probate court handles it through a protective order instead.

Opening a Loved One's Estate Through the Colleton Probate Court

Section 62-3-201 generally places the first estate filing in the county of the person's domicile, the place the law treats as home, at the time of death. A Walterboro resident's estate would therefore open in Colleton's probate court. Section 15-51-41 then requires a judge's sign-off before a death or survival claim settles. When the representative sues in circuit court, Section 15-51-42(F) calls for notice to the probate court within ten days.
