How South Carolina Personal Injury Attorneys Decide Where a Claim Is Filed

Section 15-7-30, South Carolina's general venue law, lists the counties where an injury lawsuit may go to trial. Venue just means the county whose court hears the case. At Frost Law Group, the South Carolina personal injury attorneys pick the county before drafting a complaint. When a suit starts in the wrong county, Section 15-7-100 lets the court send it elsewhere, which adds a round of motions.

State court offers two trial courts for an injury claim. Section 22-3-10 limits a magistrate's civil cases to claims of $7,500 or less in damages. Anything larger belongs in the Court of Common Pleas, and each of the eight counties holds that court in its county seat. For Dorchester County, that means St. George, even when the wreck happened in the Dorchester County part of Summerville. Frost Law Group brings car, truck, motorcycle, rideshare, slip and fall, dog bite and wrongful death claims in those courts.

Four Places the Venue Statute Can Point

The statute offers a set of possible counties, and a lawyer tests each one against the facts. Depending on who is sued, the options can include the defendant's home county, the county where the harm happened and sometimes the injured person's own county. A lawsuit with several defendants adds a fourth route. Which options apply turns on facts a lawyer has to confirm, beginning with each defendant's address on the day of the crash.

The Defendant's Home County

For a South Carolina resident, Section 15-7-30(C) allows suit in the county where that person lived when the claim arose. Section 15-7-30(E) points to a South Carolina company's principal place of business, meaning the in-state office its officers direct it from. Under Section 15-7-30(H), property ownership and business activity in a county are not enough, by themselves, to make it the principal place of business. Say a Moncks Corner driver causes a crash on Dorchester Road near Old Trolley Road. Berkeley County, the driver's home, is one available venue.

The County Where the Crash or Fall Happened

Every category in the statute includes the county where the most substantial part of the act or omission occurred. In the same Dorchester Road example, Dorchester County is the second option. For a fall in a Moncks Corner store, Berkeley County qualifies even if the owner's home office sits in another county. Claims against a government body have no other option, under Section 15-78-100(b).

The Injured Person's Own County

Only two kinds of defendants open the injured person's county as a venue. Section 15-7-30(D) covers individuals who live outside South Carolina, such as a driver from another state, and subsection (G) covers out-of-state companies that have not registered to do business here. A registered out-of-state company faces suit only where the harm happened or at its South Carolina principal place of business, under subsection (F).

Drivers From Other States on Lowcountry Interstates

In 2024 collisions statewide, 42.7% of commercial vehicle drivers came from out of state, SCDPS reports. Picture a North Carolina truck driver who rear-ends a car on I-95 in Colleton County. If the injured driver's home sits on the Dorchester County side of Summerville, a Dorchester County filing may be available alongside Colleton County.

Trucking and Delivery Companies Based Elsewhere

A tractor-trailer lawsuit may name the carrier as well as the driver. When the carrier is an out-of-state company without South Carolina registration, subsection (G) lets the injured person file at home. When it has registered, the carrier alone can be sued only in the crash county or at its in-state principal place of business. Naming an out-of-state driver in the same suit can bring the home county back into play under Section 15-7-30(B). The carrier's registration and the driver's residence both bear on where Frost Law Group can file.

Any County That Works for One of Several Defendants

Section 15-7-30(B) lets a suit against several defendants stay in any county that would work against just one of them. A chain-reaction wreck on I-26 can involve drivers from Dorchester, Berkeley and Charleston counties at once. In that setting, each driver's home county and the crash county are all candidates. Section 15-7-100 still lets the court transfer the case if the grounds for a move exist.

Government Defendants and the Tort Claims Act

A claim against the state, a county, a town or another political subdivision follows the Tort Claims Act. Section 15-78-100(b) puts those suits in the circuit court for the county where the government's act or omission happened, with no residence option. Section 15-78-120 limits recovery to $300,000 per claimant and $600,000 for any one occurrence. Under Section 15-78-110, the usual window is two years from the day the claimant learned of the loss, or should reasonably have known of it. It widens to three years for a claimant who went to the agency with a verified claim before suing. That claim counts only if it reached the agency within one year of the same date, as Section 15-78-80(d) directs. A Charleston County resident hit by a Dorchester County government vehicle on Bacons Bridge Road would ordinarily sue in Dorchester County.

Court Approval for Children's Claims and Death Claims

Some settlements need a judge's approval before money changes hands. The statutes for children's claims and death claims name which courts may approve them, and those rules differ from the general venue law. Section 62-5-433 covers claims of minors, and Section 15-51-41 covers wrongful death and survival settlements. Frost Law Group can plan for that approval step early in a case.

Settlements Over $25,000 for an Injured Child

When a child's claim nets more than $25,000, Section 62-5-433 requires approval by a circuit court. The statute names the circuit court of the county where the child lives or the county where the suit is pending. The verified petition must state that the settlement is in the child's best interests. For net amounts of $25,000 or less, the probate court can also approve, and some smaller settlements need no court approval at all.

Who Signs a Wrongful Death Suit for the Family

Only a personal representative, the person a probate court appoints to act for the estate, can bring and settle a wrongful death claim. Under Section 62-3-201, the first probate filing goes to the county where the person who died lived. Section 15-51-41 then requires a probate, circuit or federal district court to approve any wrongful death or survival settlement. The personal representative also has to notify the probate court within ten days of filing a death suit in another court.

Deadlines That Travel With the Claim, Not the County

Moving a case from one county to another does not change the filing deadline. Section 15-3-530 generally allows three years for an injury suit, and Section 15-3-535 starts that period when the injured person knew or should have known of the claim. For a child, Section 15-3-40 generally leaves the years under 18 out of the count. Even so, the extension stops one year after the child's 18th birthday at the latest. A death claim's three-year period begins on the day the person died. The controlling date turns on the facts, and the South Carolina personal injury attorneys at Frost Law Group can pin it down early.

When a Judge Moves the Trial to Another County

The county named in a complaint is not always where the trial happens. Section 15-7-100 lets a court change the place of trial for three reasons. The named county may not be proper, or there may be reason to doubt a fair and impartial trial there. A court may also move a case when the convenience of witnesses and the ends of justice call for it. Frost Law Group weighs those grounds before choosing where to file.

South Carolina has 16 judicial circuits, and each one has at least one resident circuit judge. According to the Judicial Branch, 49 circuit judges rotate through those circuits. Their terms and assignments come from the Chief Justice. Dorchester and Orangeburg share the First Circuit with Calhoun County. Charleston and Berkeley form the Ninth, a two-county circuit. Colleton and Beaufort sit in the Fourteenth with Allendale, Hampton and Jasper, the only five-county circuit in the state. Georgetown shares the Fifteenth with Horry County, and Williamsburg sits in the Third with Clarendon, Lee and Sumter.

Does a Traffic Ticket Decide Which Court Hears the Injury Claim?

No. A typical traffic ticket from a crash goes to a municipal court or a magistrate, depending on where it happened. Magistrates generally hear criminal cases carrying a fine of up to $500 or up to 30 days in jail. The injury claim is a separate civil case, and its county comes from the venue statute. The ticket and the injury claim move on separate calendars.
