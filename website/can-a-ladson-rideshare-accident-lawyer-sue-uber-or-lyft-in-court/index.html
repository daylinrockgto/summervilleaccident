Can a Ladson Rideshare Accident Lawyer Sue Uber or Lyft in Court?

Rideshare accidentsBy Tara L. FrostPublished November 16, 2026

Yes against the at-fault driver, and usually not against Uber or Lyft directly. A Ladson rideshare accident lawyer can sue a negligent rideshare driver in Berkeley or Charleston County, where the app's insurance stands behind that driver. A claim aimed at the company itself meets the arbitration clause in the rider's app terms, which sends it to an arbitrator.

Who Can You Sue After an Uber or Lyft Crash in Ladson?

You can sue the driver whose negligence caused the crash, whether that is the rideshare driver or another motorist. South Carolina's Transportation Network Company Act, Sections 58-23-1610 to 58-23-1720, requires insurance behind the rideshare driver and says nothing about arbitration. Uber and Lyft can also be named, but their own app terms push direct claims against them toward an arbitrator. The rideshare accident attorneys at Frost Law Group sort out those targets first, because that choice decides where the case is heard.

Insurance is the reason the driver matters most. Under Section 58-23-1630(C), a prearranged ride carries at least $1 million in primary liability coverage for death, bodily injury and property damage. Section 58-23-1610(9) starts that ride when the driver accepts the request and ends it when the last rider leaves the car. Section 58-23-1630(D) covers a driver whose own policy lapsed or falls short of the required coverage. The company's insurer then covers the claim from the first dollar and defends it.

A rideshare crash near Ladson can involve several possible defendants, and each one points to a different forum.

  • The rideshare driver can be sued in court, with the coverage tier set by the app status under Section 58-23-1630.
  • Another motorist who caused the crash can be sued in court, and Section 38-77-140 sets the floor for a South Carolina policy at $25,000 per person.
  • Uber or Lyft can be named for the company's own conduct, but a rider's app terms usually route that claim to arbitration.

Whether any claim reaches a jury depends on the terms you accepted, who was hurt and facts a lawyer has to review.

What Uber's Arbitration Agreement Says About Injury Claims

Uber's U.S. Terms of Use, last modified November 4, 2026, send disputes “in any way arising between you and Uber” to “binding individual arbitration.” That means a decision outside any court of law. The covered disputes expressly include “incidents or accidents resulting in personal injury or death to you or anyone else.” The clause ties those incidents to your use of the app, or someone else's use at your invitation. Uber defines itself to include its subsidiaries, representatives, affiliates, officers and directors. The terms also reach disputes that arose before you agreed to them.

In arbitration, a private arbitrator decides the claim instead of a Berkeley or Charleston County jury. That changes the setting, not the injury law. Frost Law Group's page on Uber passenger injury claims explains which policy pays. Whether Uber's clause controls a given Ladson claim turns on the version you accepted and on facts a lawyer reviews.

Riders, Guests and Bystanders Under the Uber Clause

Uber's terms bind the account holder, and they try to reach further. Section 2(a)(5) says the agreement “shall include any claims brought by or against any third parties, including but not limited to your guests.” It reaches claims that “arise out of or relate to your use of the Services or their use of the Services at your invitation.” Whether a court enforces that against someone who never clicked agree is a separate question. The Supreme Court of South Carolina wrote in Zabinski v. Bright Acres Associates (2001) that “a party cannot be required to submit to arbitration any dispute which he has not agreed to submit.”

Guests Riding on Someone Else's Account

Say your sister books an Uber for you from her phone after an event at Exchange Park. South Carolina's TNC Act still treats you as a passenger. Section 58-23-1610(6) includes a person whose ride was arranged by someone else. Uber may point to its guest language, and you may answer that you never accepted the terms. Uber's terms give an arbitrator the power to decide how the clause applies, but a court usually decides first whether you agreed at all. Keep the booking screenshots for that fight.

Pedestrians and Drivers Who Never Accepted the Terms

A pedestrian crossing College Park Road or a driver rear-ended on Highway 78 usually has no agreement with Uber or Lyft. Under Zabinski, arbitration rests on consent, so a person who never used the app usually has no clause for the company to enforce. There is one wrinkle. Uber's clause covers disputes “in any way arising between you and Uber.” A bystander who holds an Uber account for other reasons may see Uber raise it. That bystander can answer that Uber's injury clause covers incidents tied to the account holder's own use of the app. How that argument fares depends on the facts and the terms that person accepted.

Claims That Stay Out of Uber Arbitration

Uber's terms leave several kinds of claims out of arbitration. Most are narrow, but each can matter to a rider in the right case. Section 2(b) of the terms says the arbitration agreement does not require arbitration of these three.

  • Individual claims brought in small claims court stay out of arbitration “so long as the matter remains in such court and advances only on an individual basis.”
  • Individual claims of sexual assault or sexual harassment connected with your use of the app stay out of arbitration.
  • Requests for court orders to prevent the infringement or misuse of copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets, patents or other intellectual property can go to court.

Section 2(a)(1) calls these three the only exceptions to Uber's arbitration agreement. A separate clause in Section 4 still matters in almost every crash, because it covers the person behind the wheel.

A Suit Against the Driver Alone

Section 4 of Uber's terms applies its arbitration rules in place of other providers' terms, with one exception. That exception covers “claims that are solely against the Third-Party Provider,” and Section 3 lists “independent drivers” among those providers. The wording supports keeping a suit against the driver alone in court. Section 2(a)(5)'s third-party language gives the defense an argument the other way. Naming Uber beside the driver invites a motion to compel arbitration, so the choice of defendants is a strategy call a lawyer makes with you.

Small Claims and Sexual Assault Exceptions

Two of the Section 2(b) exceptions keep certain Ladson claims in a courtroom. Each one has limits set by state or federal law. Neither fits most serious injury cases from a crash on I-26 or Highway 78. Both are still worth knowing before anyone signs a release or answers an arbitration demand.

Magistrate Small Claims Courts and the $7,500 Ceiling

South Carolina magistrates hear personal injury claims when the damages claimed do not exceed $7,500, under Section 22-3-10(2). Berkeley County runs a Small Claims South magistrate office at 653 Red Bank Road in Goose Creek. Charleston County's North Area Small Claims Court sits at 4045 Bridge View Drive. A claim worth more than $7,500 does not fit there, so this exception helps mainly with minor injuries or property losses.

Sexual Assault and Harassment Claims Under Federal Law

Public Law 117-90, signed March 3, 2022, amended the Federal Arbitration Act. Under 9 U.S.C. 402(a), a person alleging sexual assault or sexual harassment may choose to make an arbitration agreement signed before the dispute unenforceable for that case. Section 402(b) says a court, not an arbitrator, decides whether that rule applies. Uber's and Lyft's terms both carve out these claims as well.

Where an Uber Arbitration Takes Place and Which Law Applies

Uber's terms say a U.S. resident's arbitration “will be conducted in the county where you reside.” For a Ladson rider, that means the county of the rider's home address, often Berkeley or Charleston. Another clause says personal injury claims are governed by the laws of the state where the incident or accident occurred. So South Carolina's fault rules still decide the claim, even without a jury. What an arbitrator awards depends on the evidence and the injuries, which a lawyer reviews with you.

How Lyft's Terms Treat a Rider's Injury Claim

Lyft's Terms of Service, dated February 9, 2026, also require arbitration of “all Claims” between you and Lyft. The listed claims include “all other federal and state statutory and common law claims,” and a negligence claim is a common law claim. Unlike Uber's version, Lyft's list does not use the words personal injury. Lyft's arbitration agreement says the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. sections 1 to 16, governs it. The American Arbitration Association runs the case under its Consumer Arbitration Rules.

Lyft's terms share some features with Uber's and differ in others.

  • Individual small claims within a small claims court's jurisdiction stay out of arbitration. That holds only while the case is not removed or appealed to a court of general jurisdiction.
  • Individual claims of sexual assault or sexual harassment tied to the Lyft platform stay out as well.
  • Claims excepted from arbitration must be filed in the state or federal courts of the state where the incident or accident occurred.
  • The agreement names Lyft's service providers, such as background check providers and payment processors, as intended third-party beneficiaries, and it does not name drivers.

Lyft's opt-out window is written for drivers. A driver who never had an earlier chance to opt out can email Lyft a signed, dated notice within 30 days after accepting the agreement. Riders get no matching window in that section. The Lyft passenger claims the firm handles still start with the TNC Act's coverage rules, which apply whichever forum hears the claim against the company.

Does South Carolina Law Keep Injury Claims Out of Arbitration?

Only when state arbitration law governs the agreement. Section 15-48-10 of the South Carolina Uniform Arbitration Act says the act does not apply to “any claim arising out of personal injury, based on contract or tort.” That exemption loses force when the Federal Arbitration Act governs instead. Both Uber's and Lyft's terms say it does.

The federal act reaches a written arbitration provision in “a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” Under 9 U.S.C. 2, that provision is “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable,” subject to the defenses that can undo any contract. How that review comes out depends on the facts and the version of the terms, which a lawyer has to read.

How Dean v. Heritage Healthcare Applied the Federal Act

The Supreme Court of South Carolina decided Dean v. Heritage Healthcare of Ridgeway on June 18, 2014. A daughter, acting for her mother's estate, brought wrongful death and survival claims against a nursing home. The circuit court refused to send them to arbitration. The Supreme Court reversed and sent the case back for the circuit court to decide the remaining defenses. Relying on the U.S. Supreme Court's Allied-Bruce decision, it explained that the federal act applies whenever an arbitration agreement involves interstate commerce, unless the parties agreed otherwise. A footnote noted that the Court's 1993 Timms v. Greene decision could not compel arbitration under state law, because the state act excludes personal injury claims. Dean then overruled Timms in its entirety. Expect Uber and Lyft to argue that an app run across state lines involves interstate commerce.

The First-Page Notice Rule Federal Law Displaces

Section 15-48-10(a) adds a second state protection. Under the state act, the first page of the contract must carry a notice in underlined capital letters or a prominent rubber stamp. Without it, the contract is not subject to arbitration under that chapter. In Soil Remediation Co. v. Nu-Way Environmental (1996), the Supreme Court of South Carolina said the federal act preempts that notice rule when the federal act covers the agreement. So a missing first-page notice on an app screen usually does not defeat a clause that federal law governs.

Uninsured Motorist Claims Stay in Court

Your uninsured motorist claim follows a different rule. Section 38-77-200 says the uninsured motorist provision “may not require arbitration of any claim arising under it.” It also bars any restriction on hiring a lawyer or filing suit. The TNC Act requires uninsured motorist coverage in both app-on tiers, under Section 58-23-1630(B)(2) and (C)(2).

Section 15-48-10(b)(4) separately exempts claims of “any insured or beneficiary under any insurance policy.” In Cox v. Woodmen of the World Insurance Co. (2001), the Court of Appeals concluded that this insurance language “reverse pre-empts” the federal act through the McCarran-Ferguson Act. Cox itself still went to arbitration, because a separate insurance statute kept that exemption from reaching fraternal benefit societies like Woodmen. Frost Law Group's page on uninsured motorist coverage in South Carolina explains how that coverage pays when the at-fault driver has none.

Which Court Hears a Ladson Rideshare Accident Lawyer's Lawsuit?

A Court of Common Pleas hears it, usually in the county where the crash happened or where a South Carolina driver lived when the claim arose. Section 15-7-30(C) allows either county for a defendant domiciled in South Carolina. For a driver domiciled elsewhere, Section 15-7-30(D) allows the county where the most substantial part of the act occurred. It also allows the county where you lived when the claim arose. Ladson has no town government, so the exact crash spot often decides the county. Frost Law Group's page for Ladson car accident lawyers covers that county line in detail.

The Census Bureau's address geocoder shows how quickly the answer changes. It places 200 College Park Road, Ladson, in Berkeley County. Exchange Park, at 9850 Highway 78 in Ladson, comes back in Charleston County. And 3800 Ladson Road, which carries a Ladson mailing address, sits inside the city limits of North Charleston.

Crashes on the Berkeley County Side of Ladson

A suit filed in Berkeley County goes to the Berkeley County Courthouse at 300-B California Avenue in Moncks Corner, in the Ninth Judicial Circuit. The Judicial Branch's Berkeley County courthouse listing confirms that address. Outside town and city limits, Section 56-5-1260 sends the crash notice to the county sheriff or the nearest Highway Patrol office. Frost Law Group handles claims at Moncks Corner through its Berkeley County injury practice.

Crashes on the Charleston County Side of Ladson

Charleston County cases go to the Charleston County Courthouse at 100 Broad Street, also in the Ninth Judicial Circuit. Exchange Park sits on this side, so a rideshare crash on Highway 78 near the fairgrounds can land there. When a Ladson crash happens inside North Charleston's city limits, Section 56-5-1260 sends the notice to the local police department instead. The firm's Charleston County injury attorneys file at Broad Street, and its guide on how to get a South Carolina accident report covers each agency's records.

What to Save Before Uber or Lyft Raises Arbitration

A motion to compel arbitration comes after a lawsuit names the company, and it rests on the terms you accepted. The records that answer it are probably on your phone right now. South Carolina's TNC Act requires some of them, which makes them easier to find later.

Gather these before you talk with any insurer about the crash.

  • Save the electronic receipt, which must list the origin, destination, total time and distance, total fare and driver's first name under Section 58-23-1660(A)(3).
  • Note whose account booked the ride, because Uber's guest language turns on that fact.
  • Write down roughly when you opened your Uber or Lyft account, since both companies revise their terms over time.
  • Record what the driver said about the app. Section 58-23-1630(H) requires the driver, on request, to tell directly interested parties, insurers and police whether the driver was logged on or on a prearranged ride.
  • Mark the nearest cross street, since the county line around Ladson can decide the courthouse.

None of these items settles the arbitration question alone. Together they let a lawyer test whether a clause applies to you before anyone signs anything.

Questions Ladson Riders Ask About Suing Uber and Lyft

These questions come up after Uber and Lyft crashes on Highway 78, College Park Road and I-26 around Ladson. Each answer names the rule behind it, so you can check the source yourself. Your own claim still turns on the terms you accepted and the facts of the crash.

Can I Opt Out of Uber's Arbitration Agreement?

Not in the usual way under the November 4, 2026, terms. Uber's terms describe a 30-day opt-out only after an arbitrator finds that a party violated the mass action waiver. That written notice must be signed by you, not by an attorney.

Does Arbitration Change the Three-Year Deadline in South Carolina?

Section 15-3-530(5) gives three years for most injury actions in South Carolina. Nothing in the TNC Act extends that period because a claim might go to arbitration. A lawyer tracks the date from the crash, whatever the forum.

What if I Was Partly at Fault for the Rideshare Crash?

You can still recover if your negligence is not greater than the combined negligence of the defendants. Nelson v. Concrete Supply Co. (1991) set that rule, and your award shrinks by your share. Uber's terms apply the law of the state where the accident occurred, so the same rule follows the claim into arbitration. Read how comparative negligence works in South Carolina for the details.

What if a Teenager Was Riding Alone in the Uber?

Uber's terms bar an account holder from letting anyone under 18 ride without an adult, apart from teen accounts for ages 13 to 17. A child's own injury claim also gets extra time under Section 15-3-40, and that added time ends one year after the child turns 18. Bring the account details to a lawyer, since who accepted which terms can change the arbitration analysis.
