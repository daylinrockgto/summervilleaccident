What Motorcycle Helmet Law Injury Lawyers in Summerville Say Section 56-5-3660 Requires

Section 56-5-3660 makes it unlawful for anyone under 21 to "operate or ride upon a two-wheeled motorized vehicle" without a protective helmet. Frost Law Group begins every helmet question with that sentence, because each phrase in it sets a separate condition. The age limit reaches the person steering and the person on the back seat alike. The helmet must also be a type the Department of Public Safety, or DPS, approves.

Two design rules follow in the same section. The helmet must carry either a neck strap or a chin strap, and it must be reflectorized, meaning fitted with reflective material, on both sides. Riders 21 and older appear nowhere in the section, and no South Carolina statute requires them to wear a helmet. An adult heading bareheaded down Ashley River Road breaks no helmet law at all.

Department of Public Safety Helmet Lists and the Sales Ban

The statute names no brand and no federal standard. Instead, it lets the department set helmet specifications by regulation and keep a list of approved helmets. Section 56-5-3690 then makes it unlawful to sell or distribute rider helmets, goggles or face shields unless the item is an approved type on that list. For a rider under 21, whether the helmet matched an approved type may come up in a claim. A young rider should save the helmet, its box label and any receipt.

Mopeds and the Words Two-Wheeled Motorized Vehicle

The helmet section never uses the word motorcycle. Its duty reaches any "two-wheeled motorized vehicle." Separately, Section 56-5-50 applies Chapter 5 to mopeds, except for the articles on vehicle equipment and vehicle condition. The helmet rules sit in Article 29, outside those two exceptions. On a plain reading, a 19-year-old riding a two-wheeled moped on Old Trolley Road falls under the same section as one on a motorcycle.

The collision report form lists motorcycles and "other motorbikes" as separate vehicle types, and the Fact Book gives the second group its own table. In 2024, officers recorded 265 of the 381 people riding those machines in collisions as wearing no helmet. Of the 22 killed, 14 had none. The Fact Book does not say whether that group includes mopeds, and its county tables list moped operators on a row of their own.

Where Does a Young Rider's Helmet Ticket Go?

Goggles or a Face Shield for Operators Under 21

Section 56-5-3670 adds eye protection, and it reaches only the operator. A rider under 21 at the handlebars needs goggles or a face shield of a type DPS approves. A passenger under 21 has no matching duty in the statute's text. SCDMV's motorcycle page tells drivers under 21 to wear a helmet and eye protection and does not mention the windscreen exemption in Section 56-5-3680.

Windscreens That Take the Place of Goggles

Section 56-5-3680 lifts the goggle rule for an operator whose motorcycle carries a windscreen meeting the department's specifications. That turns the bike into part of the proof. A 20-year-old operator with a qualifying windscreen and no goggles may have broken no eye protection rule at all. Why Riders Under 18 and Riders 18 to 20 Follow Different Rules

The helmet statute treats everyone under 21 as one group, but other statutes divide that group at 18. South Carolina's Probate Code defines a minor as a person under 18, apart from a married or emancipated teenager. A 19-year-old falls under the helmet rule, yet the deadline and settlement rules written for children no longer apply. Every deadline calculation starts with the rider's birth date and the crash date.

A Teenage Rider's Filing Deadline Under Section 15-3-40

Most injury suits carry a three-year filing window under Section 15-3-530. For a rider under 18, Section 15-3-40 keeps the years of childhood from counting, with a firm limit. The extra time can never reach past one year after the rider's 18th birthday. For a rider hurt at 17, the ordinary three years usually runs later than that cutoff, so the extra time adds nothing. The exact date depends on the birth date and on when the claim accrued, and a lawyer should confirm it early.

Court Review of a Settlement for a Minor Rider

Section 62-5-433 sets the approval steps when a child settles an injury claim. A settlement worth more than $25,000 net to the child needs circuit court approval. The petitioner must swear to the petition and state that the deal serves the child's interests. The approval order routes the money through a conservator or a probate court's protective order. A court appoints a conservator to manage a child's money, and a protective order puts the money under the probate court's control. Claims of $25,000 or less follow lighter rules. A parent alone may settle a claim that does not exceed $2,500, with no court order and no conservator.

A Civil Evidence Rule for Seat Belts but None for Helmets

South Carolina answered the seat belt version of this question in Section 56-5-6540(C). Under that subsection, a belt violation counts as neither negligence per se nor contributory negligence. Contributory negligence is the injured person's own share of carelessness. The helmet sections contain no sentence like it, for or against the rider. Evidence Carried in the Helmet Itself

A damaged helmet records the impact in a way no witness can. The outer shell, the foam liner and the strap each hold different information. Keep all three together, uncleaned and unrepaired, and let a lawyer arrange any inspection. Photos taken early and a written record of everyone who has held the helmet keep it usable as evidence in a claim.

Scrapes and Cracks on the Outer Shell

Abrasion on the shell can show which side of the head met the pavement or the car. A crack or flattened patch may mark the hardest blow. An accident reconstructionist can compare those marks with the damage on the vehicle to show how the rider moved at impact. Photos from every angle, taken before much handling, preserve that record.

The Foam Liner and a Chin Strap That Held or Failed

A dent in the foam liner under the shell may line up with the injury a physician recorded. The strap matters too, since Section 56-5-3660 requires a neck or chin strap on a young rider's helmet. A torn strap or an open buckle may help show whether the helmet stayed on. An insurer may argue the strap was loose, so the buckle belongs with the rest of the helmet.

Report Entries and Medical Charts Behind a Brain Injury Claim

Two sets of records carry most of the proof in a head injury case. The first comes from the officer at the scene, and the second comes from the hospital and the doctors who follow the rider afterward. A lawyer checks each set against the other. A mismatch gives an insurer room to argue that the head injury came from somewhere else.

Head Injury and Helmet Boxes on Form TR-310

Investigating officers in South Carolina document crashes on Form TR-310. For riders of two-wheeled or three-wheeled motorized vehicles, the form has a head injury box marked yes or no. Its safety equipment codes include one for a helmet, alongside protective pads, reflective clothing and lighting. A notice on the form says it is "for statistical reporting purposes only" and disclaims any warranty of factual accuracy.

Hospital Charts From the First Hours Onward

Medical proof of a brain injury builds over time. The first chart records what the emergency team saw on arrival. Later records show which symptoms stayed with the rider and how they affected work and home life. Gaps between those records invite an insurer's challenge.

Emergency Imaging and Trauma Team Notes

Scans taken on arrival and the trauma team's notes set the baseline. They record how alert the rider was, whether the rider lost consciousness and what the imaging showed. The complete chart, including the ambulance report, tells more than a discharge summary alone.

Neurology Visits, Therapy and Work Limits

Memory trouble, headaches or mood changes may send a rider to neurology follow-up and therapy. A treating physician's notes on work restrictions connect the injury to lost income. Records that start soon after the crash and continue without long gaps leave an insurer less room to blame something else.

Passenger Helmet Rules Under South Carolina Law for a Summerville Rider's Attorney

Who breaks the helmet law when a 19-year-old climbs on the back of a friend's motorcycle bareheaded? Section 56-5-3660 places the duty on "any person under the age of twenty-one" who operates or rides, so the text points at the young passenger. It does not name an older operator who carries that passenger. Frost Law Group keeps to that wording and does not read in duties the statute never states.

Where a passenger may sit comes from Section 56-5-3630. The motorcycle must be designed to carry more than one person. The passenger uses a seat built for two or another seat firmly attached behind or beside the operator. Passengers ride astride and facing forward, and they must be able to reach the footrests with both feet. Under subsection (D), no one may ride in a position that interferes with the operator's control or view.

Does an Older Operator Answer for a Teen Passenger's Missing Helmet?

The helmet statute does not say so. Its duty runs to the rider under 21, and nothing in the helmet sections shifts that duty to the operator. Whether the operator's choice to carry a bareheaded passenger matters in a civil claim is a question those sections leave open. Subsection (D) of Section 56-5-3630 is different. It speaks to the operator directly and bars carrying anyone in a position that interferes with control or view. A lawyer weighs both statutes against what actually happened on the bike.

The At-Fault Driver's Liability Coverage

Section 38-77-140 sets South Carolina's liability minimum at 25/50/25. At that floor, one injured person can draw no more than $25,000 in bodily injury coverage. Everyone hurt in the crash shares no more than $50,000. A passenger with a serious brain injury can run past those figures. Coverage beyond the driver's policy may then decide how much of the loss gets paid.

The Motorcycle's Policy and Policies in the Passenger's Home

A guest on the motorcycle may count as a Class II insured under the bike's policy. In Concrete Services, decided in 1998, the South Carolina Supreme Court described that class as permissive users and guests, and it held that they cannot stack. Stacking means combining underinsured limits from more than one vehicle. The same passenger may be a Class I insured under a car policy at home. Class I takes in the policyholder named on the declarations page, that person's spouse and relatives who share the home. Which policy pays, and in what order, depends on wording a lawyer compares line by line.
