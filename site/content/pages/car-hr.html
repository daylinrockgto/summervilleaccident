How Hit and Run Accident Lawyers in Summerville Open an Uninsured Motorist Claim

The declarations page, the summary sheet at the front of your auto policy, lists your uninsured motorist limits in dollars. Uninsured motorist limits cannot fall below the liability minimums in Section 38-77-140. The Three Proof Routes a Summerville Hit and Run Lawyer Uses Under Section 38-77-170

Effective May 20, 2024, Act No. 180 rewrote Section 38-77-170, the statute that controls uninsured motorist claims against unknown drivers. Before the rewrite, the statute had no recording option and no deposition alternative for a witness who refused to sign an affidavit. A claim now needs three things. You, or someone acting on your behalf, must notify police within a reasonable time, and one of three proof routes must apply. Last, your own negligence cannot be the reason the driver went unidentified.

Route One, Physical Contact With the Fleeing Car

Physical contact is the plainest route. Paint transfer on your door or a crushed quarter panel can show that another car touched yours. So can a piece of the other car's mirror lying in the road. Photograph the damage before repairs, and keep any part of the other vehicle left at the scene. A body shop can also hold replaced parts that still carry the other car's paint, if someone asks in time.

Route Two, a Witness Who Signs an Affidavit

The second route needs a witness other than the owner or driver of your car. That witness signs an affidavit, a written statement sworn under oath that describes the crash. The statute excludes only the owner and the operator of the insured vehicle. So a passenger in your car may qualify, although an insurer can still question a passenger's account.

The Capitalized Warning Printed on Every Affidavit

Section 38-77-170(B) requires the affidavit to display a warning prominently on its face. The warning, in capital letters, says a false statement about the facts may bring criminal penalties. A lawyer checks that the warning appears before the witness signs, because the statute makes it part of the document.

A Rule 27 Deposition When the Witness Will Not Sign

Some witnesses talk at the scene and then refuse to sign anything. If you cannot get the affidavit, the 2024 rewrite offers another path. You may ask a court to order the witness's deposition under the procedures in Rule 27 of the South Carolina Rules of Civil Procedure. A deposition is sworn testimony given in answer to questions, and this one happens before anyone files a lawsuit.

Route Three, a Recording of the Crash

The newest route accepts a recording "obtained electronically or otherwise" that shows the unknown vehicle caused the harm. The statute does not limit the source. Dashcam files, phone video, and doorbell or store security footage can all qualify if they capture the moment. The video has to show the other car causing the injury or damage, not just a car nearby.

Dashcam Video From Your Car or a Passing Driver

Many dashcams record on a loop and overwrite older clips, so copy the file from the memory card the same day. A passing driver's camera may have caught the plate or the impact from a better angle. Under Section 56-5-1270, an officer's written report must list the witnesses the officer interviewed, and those names are the place to start asking.

Security Cameras on Nearby Businesses and Homes

Storefront and doorbell cameras near the crash may have recorded the other car. Some systems keep footage only briefly before recording over it, so the request should go out quickly. A lawyer can send the owner a written request to preserve the file while the claim develops.

Can Your Insurer Say You Should Have Caught the Plate?

The third condition in Section 38-77-170 asks whether you were negligent in failing to identify the other car and driver. Negligence means failing to act with reasonable care, so the question is what a careful person could have done at that moment. A driver dazed by an airbag faces different circumstances than a bystander on the sidewalk. An insurer may still argue that you could have read the plate.

Why a Summerville Hit and Run Attorney Asks When and How You Reported the Crash

Picture a driver who taps your rear bumper at a traffic signal on Main Street and disappears toward Interstate 26. You pull over shaking, check on your passenger, and drive home. Section 38-77-170 requires a police report "within a reasonable time," measured against all the circumstances of the crash. It names no fixed number of hours, such as 24 or 72. Paperwork That Follows the Police Report

A hit and run can put three state forms in play, and Frost Law Group can track the two that carry their own 15-day deadlines. The FR-10 verifies insurance, and the FR-309 covers crashes no officer investigated. The FR-50 orders a copy of the finished report. Missing either deadline can cause problems that have nothing to do with the fleeing driver.

Returning the FR-10 Within 15 Days

The investigating officer gives each driver or owner a written insurance verification form, at the scene or as soon as possible afterward. The Highway Patrol calls it the mandatory insurance verification form, or FR-10. Section 56-9-350 requires the owner or driver to return it to SCDMV, the state Department of Motor Vehicles. The 15 days run from the day the officer handed over the form. A form that never comes back is prima facie evidence, meaning proof enough unless someone shows otherwise, that your vehicle had no insurance.

Filing Form FR-309 When No Officer Investigated

Section 56-5-1270 applies when no officer investigated a crash that hurt or killed someone or did $1,000 or more in apparent property damage. The driver or owner must send SCDMV a written report with proof of insurance on Form FR-309. The deadline is 15 days after the crash. Treat that filing as a separate duty from the police report Section 38-77-170 requires.

Ordering the Full Collision Report

SCDMV notes that its official collision report carries more detail than what drivers get at the scene. An official copy is available only after SCDMV receives and processes it. Two routes are common, and the one you use can depend on which agency worked the crash.

Summerville Police Reports Through CrashDocs

Leaving the Scene Penalties and the Civil Claim a Summerville Accident Lawyer Brings

A conviction for leaving the scene punishes the driver who fled. It is not the same thing as compensation for your losses. Your civil claim is a separate case that seeks money for those losses. It may run against the driver or against your own uninsured motorist coverage.

The Three Penalty Tiers in Section 56-5-1210

When a crash injures or kills someone, each driver involved must stop at the scene or as close as possible. The driver must stay until meeting the duties in Section 56-5-1230. If the injury falls short of great bodily injury, leaving is a misdemeanor. The penalty is 30 days to one year in jail, a fine of $100 to $5,000, or both.

Great bodily injury, under the statute's definition, means injury that creates a substantial risk of death. The definition also covers serious, permanent disfigurement and prolonged loss or impairment of a body part or organ's function. When great bodily injury results, leaving becomes a felony with 30 days to 10 years in prison and a fine of $5,000 to $10,000. When a death results, the felony carries one to 25 years and a fine of $10,000 to $25,000. SCDMV must revoke the license of anyone convicted under the section.

Duties the Driver Skipped by Leaving

Section 56-5-1230 lists what a driver owes after an injury crash. The driver must give a name, address, and vehicle registration number. The driver must also show a license on request and give reasonable help to anyone hurt. That help includes taking the person to a doctor or hospital, or arranging the trip, when the person plainly needs treatment or asks.

A driver who hits a parked, unattended car has a separate duty under Section 56-5-1240. That driver must notify the owner or leave a written notice in the car with names, addresses, and what happened.
