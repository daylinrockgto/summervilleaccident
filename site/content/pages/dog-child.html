The One Year After 18 Rule a Summerville Child Injury Lawyer Explains

How long does a family have to sue over a child's bite? For adults and children alike, Section 15-3-530 sets out a three-year limit on injury suits. Section 15-3-535 runs that period from discovery, and a bite is usually obvious from the day it happens. Section 15-3-40 of the South Carolina Code then adds a rule for anyone hurt before turning 18. The years spent under 18 generally do not count, and lawyers call that pause tolling. The extension cannot run longer than one year after the disability, here the child's minority, ends.

In practice, that cap draws a line at age 16. Frost Law Group can work out the exact date for each child from two facts, the birth date and the bite date. The extra time belongs to the child. Whether it reaches any claim a parent holds in the parent's own right is a separate question, so the cautious course counts such a claim from the bite itself. Any deadline depends on facts such as when the injury came to light, so a lawyer confirms it instead of trusting a formula.

Counting the Deadline From the Child's Age at the Bite

Two dates compete. One falls three years after the bite. The other is the 19th birthday, the latest point the child's extra time can reach. Whichever date comes later generally controls. Section 15-3-40 only adds time, and it never cuts the ordinary three years short. A lawyer checks both dates against the child's date of birth and the date of the attack.

Bites Before Age 16

Picture a girl bitten on her 8th birthday, in June 2026. Three years from the bite would end in June 2029. Section 15-3-40 generally carries her deadline to her 19th birthday instead, in June 2037. For a child bitten at any age under 16, the 19th birthday generally marks the outer limit.

Bites at Age 16 or 17

Now picture a boy bitten on his 17th birthday, in June 2026. His 19th birthday arrives in June 2028, yet the ordinary three years run until June 2029. The ordinary period lasts longer, so the tolling adds nothing and June 2029 generally controls. A family that counts to age 21 has misread the statute.

Filing earlier lets a lawyer work while the medical picture and the witnesses are fresh.

Settlements Netting a Child More Than $25,000

For a child netting more than $25,000, the approving court must be a circuit court. The statute sends the petition to the circuit court where the child lives, or to the one hearing a pending suit. Three steps follow, and they end with the money under a conservator's or the probate court's control. Section 62-5-433(B) sets out each one, from the petition to the hearing to the path the money takes.

The Verified Petition and Best-Interests Statement

A verified petition is one sworn as true. The statute names the petitioner as the child's conservator, or the guardian or guardian ad litem if the court has not appointed a conservator. That filing lays out the pertinent facts about the claim, the payment and any expenses, with the reasons for approval. For a share over $25,000, it must also state the petitioner's opinion that the deal serves the child's best interests.

The Hearing and the Release That Binds the Child

The judge considers the petition and may take testimony before ruling. If the court finds the settlement proper and in the child's best interests, it issues an order approving it. That order authorizes the petitioner to collect the money and sign a receipt and release that binds the child.

Conservators, Protective Orders and Money Held by the Court

Under the approval order, the money travels through a conservator or stays under a probate court's protective order. A protective order is a court order that appoints a conservator or governs how a minor's property is handled. If neither is in place when payment arrives, the petitioner pays the money into the court until a conservator qualifies or a protective order issues. Planning for that step early can keep a child's money from sitting in the court's hands.

Smaller Settlements and Section 62-5-103 Payments

At $25,000 net or less, the rules loosen. A conservator the court has already appointed may settle without court approval. Without a conservator, the guardian or guardian ad litem must seek approval from the circuit court or the county's probate court. According to the South Carolina Judicial Branch, probate courts hear minor settlements under $25,000. On that route, payment follows Section 62-5-103, a separate statute on handing money to a minor. The right path depends on the net figure and on whether a conservator exists.

Settlements Netting $2,500 or Less

The smallest claims can skip both the court and the conservator. A parent or guardian may settle for $2,500 or less, receive the money and sign a release that binds the child. The payment itself still has to follow Section 62-5-103. A small offer still deserves a lawyer's look, because a scar or later care may cost more than the first bills suggest.

The $15,000 Yearly Limit on Payments for a Child

Section 62-5-103 caps these payments at a net total of $15,000 a year. Once yearly payments exceed that figure, the statute requires a protective proceeding, the court case that seeks a protective order. A payer who knows that a conservator exists, or that an appointment is pending, may pay the conservator. Otherwise the statute offers three destinations.

Three Places the Money May Go

Without a conservator, the payer may deliver the money to the adult who has care and custody of the child and lives with the child. It may instead go to the child's guardian. The third choice is a federally insured savings account in the child's sole name. An account under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act also qualifies, and the child receives notice of the deposit.

Spending Rules for the Adult Who Receives It

An adult who receives a child's money under Section 62-5-103 is a fiduciary, a person legally bound to use it for the child. That adult may take money only to reimburse out-of-pocket costs for goods and services the child needs. The money cannot cover a support obligation the adult already owes the child. The adult must save whatever goes unspent and hand it to the child at 18, or sooner if a court order emancipates the child.
