Breath Test Results and the .08 Line

Section 56-5-2950 requires the breath sample within two hours of the arrest. Before the test starts, a simulator check has to read within a narrow band around .08. In the criminal prosecution, a result of .08 or more allows an inference that the driver was under the influence.

A result of .05 or less creates a conclusive presumption that the driver was not. Driving at .08 or higher is also its own offense under Section 56-5-2933. Those inferences belong to the criminal case by the statute's own terms. In your civil claim, the reading, if the court admits it, is one piece of proof alongside the driving, the video and the witnesses.

Scene and Breath Site Video Under Section 56-5-2953

Section 56-5-2953 requires video of a DUI suspect at the incident site and at the breath test site. The roadside video must start no later than the officer's blue lights and must capture any field sobriety tests and the arrest. At a crash investigation where the blue lights never triggered the camera, recording must start once practicable. At the breath site, the camera records the full test and, in most cases, the 20-minute waiting period before it.

The statute makes both recordings admissible under the South Carolina Rules of Evidence in a criminal, administrative or civil proceeding, by any party. It also bars disposing of a recording until any legal proceeding involving it is finally resolved. A lawyer can ask for both recordings in writing early in the claim.

Felony DUI and Great Bodily Injury Under Section 56-5-2945

Felony DUI applies when an impaired driver does something the law forbids, or neglects a legal duty, while driving. That act or neglect must proximately cause great bodily injury or death to another person. Proximate cause means a direct, legally recognized cause. Great bodily injury means bodily injury carrying a substantial risk of death or causing serious, permanent disfigurement. It also covers a long-lasting loss or impairment of a body function. That level of harm brings a mandatory fine of $5,100 to $10,100 and 30 days to 15 years in prison.

A death raises the range to a fine of $10,100 to $25,100 and 1 to 25 years. No part of either sentence may be suspended, and probation is barred. Frost Law Group handles catastrophic injury claims from impaired-driver crashes, where the medical proof has to cover a lifetime of care.

Joint and Several Liability for the Impaired Driver

Joint and several liability lets an injured person collect the entire award from any one defendant found responsible. Since July 1, 2005, Section 15-38-15 has restricted that rule, in its older version and its current one alike. A defendant whose share of fault is under 50% usually pays only that share. The versions differ on which defendants lose that protection, so Frost Law Group can check the controlling one before weighing a claim against several defendants.

The Alcohol Exception Before 2026

Under the older subsection (F), the 50% limit did not protect a defendant whose conduct involved the use, sale or possession of alcohol. Grossly negligent conduct fell outside the limit too. A drunk driver assigned less than half the fault could still owe the entire award on the alcohol ground alone. That version still governs claims that arose before the Act took effect.

What Still Triggers Full Liability Under the 2026 Version

Act No. 42 struck the alcohol and gross negligence exceptions from subsection (F). The limit still does not apply to willful, wanton, reckless or intentional conduct. Conduct involving illegal drug use, sale or possession also stays outside it. So alcohol alone no longer makes a driver answer for the whole award. Jurors may still find that driving drunk was reckless, and that finding would restore full liability.

Bars and Restaurants a Summerville Drunk Driver Accident Attorney Can Name

Many people assume a bar never answers for what a customer does after leaving. South Carolina law has said otherwise for decades. Tobias v. Under Tobias, the adult patron who drank has no claim against the seller for his or her own injuries.

The 50% Rule for Licensees in Section 61-2-147

Under the old subsection (F), a bar whose conduct involved selling alcohol also fell outside the 50% limit. That could leave it jointly and severally liable for the whole award. If one verdict finds against both a licensee and a DUI-charged driver, the licensee becomes jointly and severally liable for half the plaintiff's actual damages.

Clear and Convincing Proof in a Two-Stage Trial

Section 15-32-520 sets the standard of proof. The injured person must show by clear and convincing evidence that the harm came from willful, wanton or reckless conduct. That is a demanding standard, stricter than the proof needed for ordinary damages. Tara L. Frost's years as a Dorchester County Magistrate Judge, which ended in August 2025, showed her how fact finders apply a standard like this one to the proof.

The Compensatory Stage Comes First

If any defendant facing a punitive claim asks, a jury trial must be bifurcated, meaning split into two stages before the same jury. In the first stage, the jury decides liability and the amount of compensatory damages, the money that repays losses. Evidence that bears only on punitive damages stays out until later. Punitive damages come up only if the jury first awards compensatory damages or nominal damages, a token award for a legal wrong.

What the Jury Weighs in the Second Stage

Section 15-32-520(E) lists factors for setting the amount. They include the driver's degree of blame, awareness, any concealment, similar past conduct and ability to pay. The list also names any criminal penalties imposed for the same conduct, which brings the DUI sentence into the jury's weighing. In a case with several defendants, each punitive award must be specific to one defendant.

How the Punitive Cap Moves for an Impaired Driver

Section 15-32-530 of the South Carolina Code generally limits punitive damages to $500,000 or three times the compensatory award, whichever is greater. The $500,000 figure moves with inflation every year, and the jury never learns about the cap. The trial court decides whether an exception applies. Act No. 42 did not amend Section 15-32-530, so the same cap rules reach crashes on both sides of January 1, 2026.

Judgment Substantially Impaired by Alcohol or Drugs

No cap applies when the defendant acted while alcohol substantially impaired his or her judgment. The same exception covers drugs other than lawfully prescribed ones taken as prescribed, and intentionally consumed glue, aerosol or other toxic vapor. Having a drink is not enough by itself, since the court looks for substantial impairment of judgment.
