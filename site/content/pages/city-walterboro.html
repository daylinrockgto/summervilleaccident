What the Fairchild Court Said About Speed and Following Distance

The trial judge had refused to let the jury consider punitive damages against the truck's driver. On review, the state's highest court sided with the Court of Appeals and found that ruling wrong. Evidence suggested the driver may have broken two statutes, and a jury could infer those violations caused the crash. Whether similar proof exists in your wreck depends on the witnesses, the damage and any data from the vehicles.

Speed Under Section 56-5-1520

Section 56-5-1520(A) forbids driving faster than is reasonable and prudent for the conditions and hazards actually present. A driver can break it while under the posted limit, because the question is the conditions, not the sign. Traffic braking for a stopped vehicle ahead is one such hazard.

Following Distance Under Section 56-5-1930

Section 56-5-1930 compares the gap a driver left with what the speed of travel, the volume of traffic and the state of the highway called for. It names no distance in feet. In Fairchild, the Court treated evidence of following too closely as a second possible violation sitting alongside the speed question.

Clear and Convincing Proof for Punitive Damages

Punitive damages punish rather than repay, and they require more proof than an ordinary claim. Section 15-32-520(D) sets the bar at clear and convincing evidence. What must be shown is willful, wanton or reckless conduct behind the harm. The Fairchild Court repeated that breaking a statute is some evidence of recklessness, not proof of it by itself.

The Cap in Section 15-32-530

Triple the compensatory award or $500,000, whichever is larger, generally marks the ceiling for a punitive award. The dollar figure rises with inflation, and the Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office calculated $739,245 for 2026, effective on publication in the State Register. Several exceptions remove or raise the cap. One applies when the trial court finds the defendant's judgment was substantially impaired by alcohol, or by a drug other than one lawfully prescribed and taken as directed.

Your Own Uninsured and Underinsured Coverage

Two coverages on your own policy can matter when the other driver's insurance is thin or missing. One is required by law, and the other is a choice you made when you bought the policy. A lawyer reads both against the facts before counting on either one. The claim runs against your own insurer, which can still dispute fault and the size of the loss.

Uninsured Motorist Coverage Is Mandatory

Every auto policy issued in South Carolina has to include uninsured motorist coverage, a requirement set out in Section 38-77-150. It applies when the at-fault driver had no liability insurance, and it can also apply when the driver cannot be identified. Your declarations page shows the limits you carry, and those limits generally cap that claim.

Underinsured Coverage and the Consent Clause Ban

Underinsured motorist coverage is optional, though every insurer selling auto policies here has to offer it under Section 38-77-160. It can pay when your damages exceed the at-fault driver's liability limits. South Carolina Medicaid's Repayment Rights

By law, a Medicaid recipient is treated as having handed SCDHHS, the state Medicaid agency, the right to recover what Medicaid paid for crash care. The agency's claim outranks other repayment claims. Medicare as a Secondary Payer

Federal law makes Medicare secondary when an auto or liability insurer is responsible for the same bills. Medicare may pay first on a conditional basis, and it expects reimbursement from a settlement or judgment. That duty applies whether or not anyone admits liability in the settlement papers. It reaches the insurer that pays and anyone who receives that payment.
